# Riba and Islamic Finance: From Usury to a Modern Financial System

> **About this document.** A consolidated account of *riba* — the Quranic prohibition
> conventionally rendered as usury or interest — and of the financial system built around
> it, from the ancient Near East to the present. It merges two research files: a
> chronological study of the prohibition itself (the earlier *Interest and Usury: Riba in
> Islamic Law and History*) and a question-driven survey of banking practice. The survey
> parts cover the conventional instruments a modern reader needs as context — derivatives,
> mandatory convertible bonds, hedge funds — and the case that leveraged finance resembles
> gambling; the mechanics of Islamic retail banking, with worked examples for houses, cars,
> and *sukuk*, the default scenarios that most sharply distinguish the two systems, the
> American providers offering them, and the fintech layer emerging around them; and the
> macroeconomic questions of money printing, systemic collapse, and how a hypothetical
> fully Islamic economy would differ.
>
> The document is organized in seventeen parts. Part One is the conventional-finance context and
> the gambling question. Part Two establishes the pre-Islamic and comparative background,
> including the Jewish and Christian bans, because Islam did not originate the prohibition and
> the comparison drives Part Seventeen. Part Three covers the Quranic material and the hadith,
> in the order the verses were revealed. Part Four is the juristic settlement, where a short
> scriptural prohibition became a technical body of law and where the schools' disagreement
> over the prohibition's rationale opened the space every later argument occupies. Part Five
> covers a millennium of practice and workaround. Part Six follows the parallel collapse of
> the Christian ban. Parts Seven and Eight trace the modern debate and the
> institutionalization of Islamic banking. Part Nine is the present state of the industry and
> its critics, and Parts Ten through Thirteen are the practice sections: Islamic banking in
> operation, why it remains niche, the American market, and the fintech layer built on top of
> it. Part Fourteen turns to money printing and systemic collapse, Part Fifteen to the
> macroeconomics of a hypothetical Islamic world, Part Sixteen to the fiscal instruments and
> the hoarding prohibitions that complete the system, and Part Seventeen sets the three
> traditions side by side and maps the positions. Researched Aug. 22 and
> Sept. 28, 2026, from academic and confessional secondary literature; primary Arabic texts
> were not consulted directly, and claims are attributed to the scholars and studies that
> make them.

# Part One: Conventional Finance and the Gambling Question

Before tracing the prohibition, it helps to see what the modern financial system actually
contains, because the classical jurists never faced anything like it and because the Islamic
response is defined largely by what it rules out. Three instruments or entities carry most of
the argument — derivatives, mandatory convertible bonds, and hedge funds — and one objection
hangs over all of them: that leveraged finance is gambling with better manners.

## 1. Derivatives: The Financial Side-Bets

A derivative is a financial contract whose value is "derived" from an underlying asset, such
as a stock, bond, commodity (like gold or oil), or interest rate. Instead of buying the
physical asset, parties enter a contract based on how that asset's price will move.

- **The analogy.** Think of buying a ticket that pays out if a specific sports team wins the
  championship. You don't own the team; you just own a contract that relies on their
  performance
- **Core mechanics.** Derivatives are primarily used for hedging (insuring against price
  drops) or speculation (betting on price movements to make a profit)

The common types:

- **Options** — the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a set price
- **Futures and forwards** — obligatory contracts to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on
  a future date
- **Swaps** — private agreements to exchange cash flows, such as swapping a variable interest
  rate for a fixed one

## 2. MCBs: The Hybrid Capital Raiser

A Mandatory Convertible Bond (MCB) is a hybrid financial instrument that starts its life as a
debt security (a bond) but automatically converts into a fixed number of shares of equity
(company stock) at a specific maturity date.

- **The difference.** Unlike a standard convertible bond, where the investor chooses whether
  or not to swap the bond for stock, an MCB forces the conversion. Investors must accept the
  stock when the bond matures, regardless of the stock price
- **Why companies issue them.** It allows a company to raise immediate cash through debt
  without immediately diluting their stock or triggering a credit rating downgrade
- **Why investors buy them.** They usually offer a higher interest rate (coupon) than standard
  convertible bonds, to compensate the investor for taking on the risk of mandatory stock
  ownership

## 3. Hedge Funds: The Elite Investment Pools

A hedge fund is a private, pooled investment vehicle open only to institutional investors and
wealthy individuals (accredited investors). They are managed aggressively using a wide array
of sophisticated financial strategies.

- **"Hedging" vs. reality.** Originally designed to hedge — protect — portfolios against
  market downturns, modern hedge funds use highly aggressive, complex tactics to chase high
  absolute returns
- **Aggressive tactics.** Unlike traditional mutual funds, hedge funds heavily utilize short
  selling (betting a stock will fall), leverage (borrowing money to amplify bets), and
  derivatives
- **Fee structure.** They traditionally operate on a "2 and 20" model — charging a 2 percent
  annual management fee on all assets under management plus a 20 percent performance fee on
  any profits generated
- **Regulation.** Because they only accept wealthy investors who are presumed to understand
  the high risks, hedge funds face far fewer regulatory restrictions than standard retail
  investment funds

At a glance:

| Instrument/Entity | Primary Purpose | Key Risk Factor | Who Uses It? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Derivatives | Risk management (hedging) or speculative profit | Extreme volatility due to leverage | Corporations, banks, and traders |
| MCBs | Corporate capital raising with delayed equity issuance | Forced conversion into low-value stock if equity drops | Large corporations and institutional buyers |
| Hedge funds | Maximizing absolute returns across all market conditions | High fees, illiquidity, and aggressive strategy failures | Ultra-high-net-worth individuals and institutions |

## 4. Investing or Gambling?

It is easy to see why all of this looks exactly like gambling. Many critics, economists, and
regulators argue that parts of the financial system — especially highly leveraged derivatives
and speculative hedge funds — behave exactly like a casino. Finance professionals draw a
sharper line, based on purpose, mathematical calculation, and economic utility.

The critical differences:

- **Risk management — the main difference.** In a casino, you create risk that didn't exist
  before for the sake of entertainment. In finance, risk already exists in the real world (a
  farmer faces the risk that crop prices will crash), and derivatives allow that farmer to
  transfer the risk to someone else — a speculator willing to take it on for a price
- **Economic value.** Gambling is a zero-sum game that creates no real-world economic growth.
  Financial markets, even speculative ones, provide liquidity (making it easy to buy and sell
  things) and price discovery (helping the world figure out what an asset is actually worth)
- **The odds.** In a casino, the house always has a mathematical edge; over time, the gambler
  is guaranteed to lose. In finance, while there are no guarantees, investors can use economic
  data, company earnings, and mathematical modeling to find an edge where the odds favor them

Where it becomes "legalized gambling": the line blurs heavily in speculation. When hedge
funds or day traders use massive leverage — borrowed money — to place large bets on short-term
price movements using derivatives, it functions like a high-stakes casino. A fund that bets
billions of dollars that a stock will drop 5 percent by Friday, using options, is not
supporting economic growth; it is making a calculated wager. If it is right, it makes a
fortune; if it is wrong, it can go bankrupt overnight.

This distinction maps almost exactly onto the jurists' categories in Part Four. Derivatives
and short selling are textbook *gharar* — contracts resting on excessive uncertainty — and
speculative side-betting fails the classical test that gain must arise from real economic
activity and assumed risk. The reader who accepts the "legalized gambling" description above
has, without using the word, accepted the traditionalist case against the modern financial
system that Part Seven's modernists would decline to make.

# Part Two: Before Islam

## 1. The Ancient Near East and the Classical World

Lending at interest is older than any surviving scripture, and so is the attempt to restrain
it. The pattern in every ancient system is the same, a cap rather than a ban.

The **Code of Hammurabi**, from around the 18th century BCE, set maximum rates by commodity,
capping silver loans at 20 percent and grain loans at 33.3 percent. That is not a prohibition.
It is a recognition that credit was economically necessary and that its price required
regulation.

**Greek** thought supplied the moral objection that Christian and Muslim jurists would later
inherit. Plato and Aristotle both treated interest as unjust, and Aristotle's formulation
proved the durable one, that money is barren and generating money from money is therefore
unnatural. Greek city-states nonetheless regulated lending pragmatically rather than banning
it.

**Rome** followed the same path. The Twelve Tables capped interest at 8 1/3 percent, and later
Roman law permitted 1 percent monthly, or 12 percent a year.

## 2. Judaism

The Torah prohibits interest in three places, at Exodus 22:25, Leviticus 25:36-37, and
Deuteronomy 23:19-20. The prohibition is unambiguous, and it is bounded. It forbids charging
interest to a fellow Israelite while permitting it in dealings with foreigners, and that
insider-outsider structure is the feature that most distinguishes the Jewish ban from the two
that followed.

Jewish law also produced the most enduring of all the workarounds, the ***heter iska***. The
device restructures what is economically a loan as a joint investment, so that the lender's
return is a share of profit in a venture rather than a charge on a debt. It is valid under
*halakha* precisely because risk is nominally shared. Anyone who reads Part Nine of this
document will recognize the structure immediately.

## 3. Christianity

Christianity inherited the Jewish suspicion of interest and removed the exemption. There was
no category of foreigner to whom lending at interest was permitted, and medieval theologians
were close to unanimous that taking interest at any rate from any person was a grave sin.

The conciliar record runs from the Council of Nicaea in 325 and the Council of Laodicea in 364,
which legislated against usury among clergy, to the Third Lateran Council of 1179, which
denied both the sacraments and Christian burial to those who took interest on loans. **Thomas
Aquinas** supplied the mature theory, arguing that usury is contrary to natural law and, with
Augustine, condemning the usurer for selling time — which belongs to God and not to the lender
— and for producing money out of money.

The Christian ban was therefore stricter in principle than the Jewish one and, for a period,
enforced with sanctions no Muslim polity ever matched. Part Six follows what became of it.

## 4. Pre-Islamic Arabia

The practice the Quran addressed has a name in the tradition, ***riba al-jahiliyya***, and the
sources describe a specific mechanism rather than a general market rate.

A debtor who could not repay at maturity was offered a choice, settle now or have the debt
doubled. A hundred dirhams unpaid became two hundred; unpaid again, four hundred. Some
accounts describe the debt as doubled and redoubled annually until it was discharged or the
debtor was ruined. What the sources describe, in other words, is a penalty-compounding regime
applied at default, and the outcome was frequently the enslavement or dispossession of the
debtor.

This matters more than any other fact in Part Two, because the whole modern argument turns on
whether the Quran prohibited *this*, or prohibited the wider genus of which this is one
species. The traditional and the modernist readings in Part Seven both begin here and diverge
immediately. The usual source-critical caution applies, that the *jahiliyya* is described in
literature written down from the late 8th century onward, in which it functions partly as a
moral foil for Islam.

# Part Three: The Quranic Prohibition, 610-632

## 1. The Four Stages, in Order

The prohibition arrived in four stages across roughly two decades, escalating from
disapproval to categorical ban. The sequence is itself an argument that both camps deploy,
and the verses are given below in revelation order rather than in the order of the text.

### Stage one, Mecca — Surah Ar-Rum 30:39

> Whatever loans you give, ˹only˺ seeking interest at the expense of people's wealth will not
> increase with Allah. But whatever charity you give, ˹only˺ seeking the pleasure of Allah —
> it is they whose reward will be multiplied.

The earliest reference, and not prohibitive in form. It sets riba against *sadaqa* as
spiritually barren against spiritually fruitful, establishing the moral frame long before any
legal rule.

### Stage two — Surah An-Nisa 4:161

> taking interest despite its prohibition, and consuming people's wealth unjustly. We have
> prepared for the disbelievers among them a painful punishment.

Part of a passage of reproach directed at the Jews. The verse presupposes that taking interest
was already forbidden to them, which links the Quranic prohibition explicitly to the Torah
material in Part Two, and it pairs riba with consuming wealth unjustly — the closest the Quran
comes to naming the wrong involved.

### Stage three — Surah Ali Imran 3:130

> O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of
> Allah, so you may prosper.

The first prohibition addressed to Muslims, and the pivotal verse for the entire modern
dispute. The Arabic phrase rendered here as multiplying it many times over is *ad'afan
muda'afa*, doubled and redoubled — the exact mechanism of *riba al-jahiliyya*. Whether that
phrase describes the prohibited thing or merely its most flagrant instance is the question
Part Seven turns on.

### Stage four, Medina — Surah Al-Baqarah 2:275-280

The final and most severe statement.

> **(275)** Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to
> madness by Satan's touch. That is because they say, "Trade is no different than interest."
> But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest. Whoever refrains — after having
> received warning from their Lord — may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to
> Allah. As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will
> be there forever.

> **(276)** Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful. And Allah does not like
> any ungrateful evildoer.

> **(278)** O believers! Fear Allah, and give up outstanding interest if you are ˹true˺
> believers.

> **(279)** If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger! But if you
> repent, you may retain your principal — neither inflicting nor suffering harm.

> **(280)** If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease.
> And if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you, if only you knew.

Four features of this passage carry most of the argumentative weight.

- **The objection is quoted and rejected.** Verse 275 puts the pro-interest argument in the
  mouths of its opponents — trade is no different than interest — and answers it by
  distinguishing the two categorically rather than by degree. Any modernist reading has to
  contend with the fact that the Quran anticipates and dismisses the analogy between profit and
  interest
- **The language is exceptionally severe.** The warning of war from God and His Messenger in
  279 has no parallel in the Quran's treatment of commercial dealing
- **The remedy is the principal.** Verse 279 leaves the lender the capital sum and nothing
  more, *la tazlimuna wa la tuzlamun*, neither wronging nor being wronged. The prohibition is
  framed as the removal of a wrong, which is what gives the modernist argument its foothold
- **Verse 280 addresses default directly.** A debtor in difficulty gets postponement, and
  remission is better still. This is the precise inverse of *riba al-jahiliyya*, where default
  triggered doubling

## 2. What the Quran Prohibits, and What It Leaves Open

**Settled on the text alone.** Riba is categorically forbidden to Muslims. It is distinguished
in kind from trade, which is permitted. Outstanding riba must be relinquished, leaving the
creditor the principal. Default calls for forbearance or remission, not for an increase. And
the wrong involved is characterized as consuming wealth unjustly.

**Not settled on the text alone.** The Quran never defines *riba*. It does not state a rate, a
threshold, or a set of covered transactions. It does not distinguish consumption loans from
commercial ones, or simple from compound charges, or inflation compensation from real return.
And it does not say whether *ad'afan muda'afa* at 3:130 restricts the prohibition or merely
describes its worst form.

That definitional gap is what Part Four exists to fill, and the manner of the filling is what
Parts Seven and Eight are still arguing about.

## 3. The Hadith and the Six Commodities

The hadith corpus supplied the definition the Quran omitted, and it did so by way of a rule
about barter rather than about lending.

The central report names six items.

> Gold for gold, silver for silver, wheat for wheat, barley for barley, dates for dates, and
> salt for salt — like for like, equal for equal, hand to hand. If these classes differ, sell
> as you wish, provided it is hand to hand.

Related reports curse the taker of riba, the giver, the witness, and the scribe, placing all
four under the same condemnation. The Farewell Sermon includes a public renunciation of riba.

The structural consequence of the six-commodities report is the one that shaped everything
after it. The prohibition was no longer only about lending at a stated increase. It now
covered any exchange of two units of the same *ribawi* commodity in unequal quantity, and any
such exchange with delayed delivery. That extension took the prohibition out of the domain of
moral exhortation about exploitation and into the domain of technical contract law, where it
has remained.

# Part Four: The Juristic Settlement, 8th-10th Centuries

## 1. The Two Ribas

The jurists split the prohibition into two categories that answer to different concerns.

| Category | Arabic | What it prohibits | Rooted in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Riba al-nasi'a* | ربا النسيئة | An increase stipulated for deferral — the classical loan at interest, and also deferred delivery in an exchange of *ribawi* goods | The Quran, and the *jahiliyya* practice |
| *Riba al-fadl* | ربا الفضل | An excess in a hand-to-hand exchange of two quantities of the same *ribawi* commodity, however small, and whatever the reason | The six-commodities hadith |

*Riba al-nasi'a* is what a modern reader recognizes as usury or interest. *Riba al-fadl* is
something no Western legal tradition ever prohibited. Trading one weight of gold for a
slightly larger weight, even where the difference reflects a genuine quality difference such
as a wrought ring against plain bullion, falls under it.

The presence of *riba al-fadl* in the law is analytically decisive, and it is routinely
skipped in popular treatments. It shows that the classical prohibition cannot be reduced to a
concern about exploiting desperate borrowers, because no borrower is involved in a spot barter
of equal-quality gold. Something else is being protected — the integrity of the measure of
value, and the exclusion of gain that does not arise from real economic activity.

## 2. The *Illa* Problem

Since only six commodities are named, the jurists had to identify the *'illa* — the operative
legal cause — in order to know what else was covered. They did not agree, and the
disagreement is the hinge of the whole subject.

| School | The *illa* identified | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hanafi | Measurability by weight or volume, combined with unity of genus | The widest extension; any fungible commodity sold by weight or measure becomes *ribawi* |
| Maliki | For the four foods, being a storable foodstuff — both conditions required; for gold and silver, their currency function | Narrower on foods; anchored in preservability |
| Shafi'i | Being a currency (for gold and silver) or being edible (for the rest) | Edibility alone suffices, so a broad class of foods is covered |
| Hanbali | Two transmitted positions, tracking the Hanafi and the Shafi'i views respectively | Internal variation; Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim emphasized the *thaman* or currency function as the efficient cause |

The rationales the jurists offered for these causes cluster around preventing hoarding-induced
scarcity, blocking speculative inequality in exchanges of like for like, and protecting the
integrity of money.

## 3. Why the *Illa* Dispute Matters

Three consequences follow, and each surfaces later in this document.

- **The scope of the prohibition was never fixed by revelation.** It was fixed by juristic
  reasoning from an undefined term and a list of six items, and the schools reached different
  answers. That is an ordinary feature of Islamic law, but it undercuts any claim that the
  boundaries of riba are self-evident from scripture
- **Whether paper money is *ribawi* depends on the *illa* chosen.** The Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn
  al-Qayyim emphasis on currency function is the classical position that most readily extends
  to fiat money, and it is the one on which the modern prohibition of bank interest most
  comfortably rests. A strict reading tied to gold and silver as metals does not extend so
  easily
- **The law became formal rather than purposive.** Once the test is whether a transaction's
  *form* matches a defined category, a transaction restructured to fall outside the category
  passes — regardless of its economic effect. That is the mechanism that produced Part Five
  and, a millennium later, the critique in Part Nine

# Part Five: Practice and Workaround, 900-1800

A prohibition on interest does not abolish the need for credit. What Muslim societies did for
the next nine centuries was build instruments that met the need, some of them genuine
alternatives and some of them evasions, and the line between the two was contested at the time.

## 1. The Instruments That Worked

These were not workarounds. They were legitimate contract forms that performed financing
functions without a stipulated increase on a debt, and they underwrote a commercial economy of
considerable sophistication.

- ***Mudaraba*** — a partnership in which one party supplies capital and the other supplies
  labor and enterprise, profits divided by prior agreement and losses borne by the capital
  provider. The Italian *commenda*, which allowed merchants to invest in trading voyages
  without sailing on them, corresponds to it closely, and the direction of borrowing is a live
  question in economic history
- ***Musharaka*** — a joint equity partnership with proportionate sharing of profit and loss
- ***Suftaja*** — the bill of exchange. A merchant at Basra could issue an instrument payable
  at Samarkand without moving silver along the caravan routes. By the 9th to 12th centuries it
  underpinned long-distance trade, and the Cairo Geniza documents record such exchanges in
  dinars and dirhams across distances exceeding two thousand kilometers
- ***Hawala*** — a trust-based remittance network moving value through agents and periodic
  clearing rather than physical cash, codified as a valid contract in Islamic legal texts from
  the 8th and 9th centuries
- ***Murabaha*** — a cost-plus sale, in which a financier buys an asset and resells it to the
  client at a disclosed markup, payable later. In its classical form a genuine sale with a real
  transfer of ownership and risk

The jurists policed these. Fees on *suftaja* and *hawala* were capped to reflect actual
services such as custody and transfer, precisely so that the charge would not become a
disguised return on a loan, and the Hanafis debated the legality of these instruments from the
10th century on grounds of *gharar* and concealed riba.

## 2. The Instruments That Evaded

The formalism identified at the end of Part Four had a predictable result. Contracts appeared
whose components were each individually lawful and whose combined effect reproduced a loan at
interest.

The archetype is ***bay' al-'ina***, the double sale. A lender sells an object to a borrower
for 110 payable in a year, then immediately buys it back for 100 in cash. Two valid sales; net
effect, a loan of 100 repaid as 110. The schools divided sharply, with the Shafi'is generally
permitting it on the ground that each sale satisfies the formal requirements and intention is
not justiciable, and the Malikis and Hanbalis rejecting it under the doctrine of blocking the
means to an unlawful end.

That eleventh-century argument is, in substance, the same argument being conducted in Part
Eight about *tawarruq*. The question has never changed. Does Islamic law regulate the form of
a transaction or its economic substance, and if the former, is a formally compliant
reproduction of a prohibited outcome permissible?

## 3. The Ottoman Cash Waqf Controversy

The most instructive institutional episode ran through the 16th-century Ottoman Empire and
went to the top of the state.

A *waqf* is a charitable endowment. A **cash waqf** endows money rather than land, and the
endowment sustains itself by lending the cash and applying the return to the charitable
purpose. Whether that return was riba was the question, and there was no evading it, because
the institutions were numerous, publicly registered, and funding mosques, schools, and
neighborhood welfare.

**Ebussuud Efendi**, as Şeyhülislam, issued fatwas between roughly 1545 and 1574 permitting
waqfs to lend at interest on grounds of necessity. He was opposed vehemently.
**Çivizade**, his predecessor in the office, argued that interest on any loan fell within the
prohibition, wrote to the sultan, and succeeded in having cash waqfs banned for two to three
years. The matter was settled politically rather than juristically, by imperial decree under
Suleiman.

Three things make this episode worth close attention. The highest religious authority of the
largest Sunni empire authorized interest-bearing lending by explicit fatwa. The dissent came
from an equally senior jurist and was not marginal. And the resolution came from the ruler, not
from the scholarly consensus that later accounts often invoke as settled. Scholarship on the
late Ottoman period traces a further shift, in which *murabaha* itself moved from an accepted
financing form toward the category of usury as the debate evolved.

## 4. Kuran's Argument and Its Critics

**Timur Kuran's** *The Long Divergence: How Islamic Law Held Back the Middle East* (Princeton,
2011) is the most influential economic account of this period, and although it is not primarily
about riba, it bears on it directly.

Kuran argues that Islamic legal institutions, beneficial to the Middle Eastern economy in the
early centuries, began from around the 10th century to slow the emergence of the central
features of modern economic life — private capital accumulation, the corporation, large-scale
production, and impersonal exchange. His principal mechanism is not the interest prohibition
but the failure to develop the corporation as a legal person separable from family and tribe.
The consequences he traces include commercial partnerships that involved few partners and
dissolved at the end of short ventures, and credit markets that remained narrow into the late
18th century. He rejects colonialism and geography as the primary explanations.

The critics answer that Kuran understates the flexibility the system actually exhibited,
pointing to the creative use of the *waqf* to work around inheritance rules and to the
tradition's demonstrated willingness — the cash waqf being the case in point — to permit
interest where it was needed. That objection is well founded on the evidence in section 3, and
it does not dispose of Kuran's argument about the corporation, which is a separate claim.

# Part Six: The Christian Ban Collapses, 1179-1830

The Christian prohibition was stricter than the Islamic one in principle, enforced with
sanctions no Muslim state imposed, and it disappeared. Tracing how is necessary background for
Part Seventeen, because the contrast is the datum any explanation has to account for.

| Date | Development |
| --- | --- |
| 1179 | The Third Lateran Council denies sacraments and Christian burial to those taking interest on loans |
| 13th c. | Aquinas grounds the ban in natural law, arguing that the usurer sells time and makes money from money |
| 1515 | The Fifth Lateran Council approves the *montes pietatis* over Cardinal Cajetan's objection, forbidding them usury but permitting charges to defray the cost of running them |
| 16th c. | Reformation-era reappraisal, distinguishing commercial lending from the exploitation of the poor |
| 1745 | Benedict XIV's *Vix Pervenit* defines and condemns usury, closing a period of bitter theological dispute |
| Aug. 18, 1830 | Pius VIII's *Datum in audientia* abolishes all ecclesiastical penalties for usury |

The decisive moment is 1515, and it is decisive for a reason that will be familiar. Once an
institution could lawfully charge borrowers to cover the cost of lending, the question became
unanswerable in principle. If the *montes* could recover employee salaries from borrowers, a
private pawnbroker could argue the same, and lending as a business had been conceded in
substance while the prohibition remained on the books. The formal ban then persisted for three
more centuries with a widening gap between doctrine and practice, until the penalties were
simply withdrawn.

The parallel with Part Five is exact. A prohibition that operates on the form of a transaction
is undone not by frontal challenge but by an authorized exception whose logic generalizes.
Whether the Islamic ban is currently at its own 1515 is the question Part Nine leaves open.

# Part Seven: The Modern Debate Opens, 1900-1970

Colonial-era banking put the question to Muslim jurists in a form the classical law had not
anticipated. Interest was no longer a moneylender's charge on a distressed debtor. It was the
organizing mechanism of a monetary system, embedded in savings deposits, government bonds,
trade finance, and the post office.

## 1. Abduh, Rida, and the Modernist Reading

**Muhammad Abduh**, grand mufti of Egypt, is reported to have permitted interest on postal
savings, and his arguments on bank interest were published in *al-Manar* in 1906. The text of
the fatwa itself is not extant, which is worth stating plainly, because it is cited more often
than it can be verified.

**Rashid Rida**, and later **Ma'ruf al-Dawalibi** and **Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri**, developed
variants of the same position. Their move was to restrict the Quranic prohibition to a defined
subset — interest charged after default, interest on consumption loans, and compound interest —
each of which maps onto *riba al-jahiliyya* as described in Part Two. On that reading a
prespecified return on a commercial deposit or a productive loan is not the thing the Quran
prohibited.

The argument has three legs. The historical leg, that 3:130's *ad'afan muda'afa* identifies the
prohibited practice as penalty compounding at default. The purposive leg, that the Quran names
the wrong as consuming wealth unjustly and as *zulm*, so where no injustice occurs the
prohibition's rationale is absent. And the institutional leg, that a modern bank depositor is
an investor in a diversified enterprise rather than a creditor squeezing a debtor.

## 2. Fazlur Rahman

**Fazlur Rahman** published "Riba and Interest" in *Islamic Studies* in March 1964, and it
remains the most rigorous statement of the modernist case. Rahman's position is that the
prohibition's ground is the exploitation of the needy rather than the interest rate as such,
and that the ruling must be read through the moral concern that generated it rather than
applied as a formal category detached from it.

Rahman's larger contribution is methodological — the double-movement hermeneutic, which reads
a ruling back to the situation and purpose that produced it and then forward to present
conditions. **Abdullah Saeed's** *Islamic Banking and Interest: A Study of the Prohibition of
Riba and Its Contemporary Interpretation* (1996) develops the method into a systematic model
that distinguishes timeless universal principles from time-bound rulings and prioritizes the
Quran's overarching moral objectives over textual literalism.

The objection to all of this is the one Part Three flagged. Verse 2:275 anticipates the analogy
between trade and interest and rejects it, and *riba al-fadl* in Part Four has nothing to do
with exploiting anyone. A theory that locates the entire ground of the prohibition in
exploitation cannot account for either.

## 3. The Revivalist Answer

The counter-position held that riba means every stipulated increase on a loan, that the
classical schools were unanimous on the substance whatever their differences over the *illa*,
and that the modernist reading substitutes a modern intuition about fairness for a revealed
category. Its practical program was not to reinterpret the prohibition but to build
institutions that could operate without violating it — which is Part Eight.

# Part Eight: Institutionalization, 1963-2000

## 1. From Mit Ghamr to Dubai

- **1963, Mit Ghamr.** Ahmad El Naggar founded the Mit Ghamr Savings Bank in the Egyptian
  Delta, modeled partly on German savings cooperatives and operating without interest. It
  attracted thousands of depositors and financed local production, especially agriculture. It
  is generally recognized as the first modern Islamic banking experiment, and it was closed for
  political rather than commercial reasons
- **1971-1972, the Nasser Social Bank.** Established as a social rather than a profit-oriented
  institution, serving low-income depositors outside the banking system, and absorbing the Mit
  Ghamr project in 1972
- **1975, the Islamic Development Bank.** Opened at Jeddah as an intergovernmental institution
  for the economic and social development of member states, giving the movement a multilateral
  anchor
- **1975, Dubai Islamic Bank.** The first full commercial Islamic bank, and the template for
  the industry that followed

## 2. The Three National Conversions

Three states attempted to convert entire financial systems rather than add Islamic
institutions alongside conventional ones.

- **Iran** passed the Usury-Free Banking Operation Law on Sept. 1, 1983, implemented March 21,
  1984, converting the banking system at a stroke. The Iranian system is distinctive in that
  compliance is a statutory requirement rather than a regulatory option
- **Sudan** legislated for fully sharia-compliant banking in 1984, then pursued full
  Islamization of the financial system through the Banking Law of 1992, which aimed to
  eliminate interest from banking and from government transactions. A Sharia Supervisory
  Council was established at the central bank in 1993, and the Business Banking Act followed in
  2003
- **Pakistan** attempted conversion through litigation as much as legislation, which is section
  4

## 3. The 1985 Resolution

The **OIC Islamic Fiqh Academy**, meeting at Jeddah for its second session from Dec. 22 to 28,
1985, resolved that any excess or profit on a loan stipulated at contract, and any excess
charged for deferral where a borrower cannot repay at maturity, are both forbidden as riba.

That resolution is the institutional settlement of the question Part Seven had opened. It closed
the modernist reading at the level of transnational scholarly authority, and every subsequent
mainstream fatwa body has proceeded from it. What it did not do was resolve the substantive
argument, which continued and continues.

## 4. Pakistan's Courts, 1990-1999

Pakistan is the only country to have litigated the question to a final judgment, and the
procedural history is long enough to be worth setting out.

A petition to abolish interest-based banking was filed in the Federal Shariat Court on June 30,
1990. A three-member bench under Chief Justice Tanzeelur Rehman delivered judgment on Nov. 14,
1991, holding the interest-based system repugnant to Islam and requiring implementation by
April 30, 1992. The Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court dismissed the appeal on Dec.
23, 1999, and ordered that laws involving interest cease to have effect by June 30, 2001.

The final draft of the 1999 judgment was written by **Justice Muhammad Taqi Usmani**, and it
was subsequently published as *The Text of the Historic Judgment on Interest*. It is the
fullest judicial statement of the traditionalist case anywhere, and it engages the modernist
arguments directly rather than dismissing them. It answers the claim that modern commercial
interest was unknown in the Prophet's time and therefore outside the prohibition; the claim
that the rationale cannot apply where debtors are wealthy and borrowing productively, so that
no injustice occurs; and the claim that riba denotes only usurious loans at excessive rates.
Anyone assessing the traditionalist position should read it rather than a summary of it,
including this one.

## 5. Tantawi and Al-Azhar

The most significant modern dissent from a mainstream institution came from Egypt's highest
religious authority.

**Muhammad Sayyid Tantawi**, Shaykh al-Azhar, held that certain forms of financial return are
lawful profit — *ribh* — rather than riba, including returns on government bonds and on ordinary
savings accounts. Asked by the International Arab Banking Corporation about deposits invested
in permissible activity with returns distributed at prespecified amounts and agreed intervals,
he issued a response on 27 Ramadan 1423, Dec. 2, 2002. The Al-Azhar Institute of Islamic
Jurisprudence issued an opinion the same year on fixed returns on bank deposits, and Ali Gomaa
subsequently addressed the question as well.

The Tantawi fatwa is the single most consequential document in the modern debate, for a
structural reason. It means the claim of contemporary consensus against bank interest is
false as stated. The most prestigious institution in Sunni Islam took the other side, publicly,
in writing, from its highest office. Whether the reasoning is sound is a separate matter, and
critics argued that the arrangement described to Tantawi was a conventional deposit dressed in
investment language. But the position exists and is not marginal, and accounts that present the
prohibition of bank interest as undisputed among mainstream authorities are inaccurate.

# Part Nine: The Present, 2000-2026

## 1. Scale

Islamic finance is now a large industry rather than a movement. Global Islamic finance assets
reached $5.2 trillion in 2025, growing 14.9 percent year on year, and are projected to approach
$6 trillion in 2026 across banking, capital markets, *takaful*, and a fast-growing Islamic
fintech sector. Sukuk issuance reached $264.8 billion in 2025, up from $234.9 billion the
previous year, with foreign-currency issuance passing $100 billion and roughly doubling its
2021 volume. Financing grew more than 17 percent in 2025 and deposits by nearly 9 percent, led
by the Gulf and Asia, with Tanzania, Zambia, and Kenya among the new entrants bringing African
markets into the sukuk system.

The industry's own reporting identifies structural challenges around liquidity management and
market diversification. The deeper challenge is the subject of the next two sections.

## 2. The Sharia Arbitrage Critique

**Mahmoud El-Gamal's** *Islamic Finance: Law, Economics and Practice* (Cambridge, 2006) is the
most serious internal critique of the industry, and it comes from an economist holding an
endowed chair in Islamic economics who has served at the IMF and as scholar in residence on
Islamic finance at the U.S. Treasury.

His argument is that the industry has placed excessive weight on contract form and thereby made
itself a target for rent-seeking legal arbitrage. Where a conventional instrument's economic
substance can be reproduced through a chain of formally compliant contracts, the resulting
product is functionally identical, costs more to produce, and delivers no distinctive Islamic
benefit — while generating fees for those who certify it. He is pointed about Sharia
Supervisory Boards, describing them as rent-seeking arbitrageurs and comparing the role to the
pre-Reformation sale of indulgence certificates.

The argument is the direct descendant of the *bay' al-'ina* dispute in Part Five. El-Gamal's
position is essentially the Maliki and Hanbali one, that blocking the means to a prohibited end
is part of the law, restated in the vocabulary of financial economics.

## 3. Tawarruq

The clearest test case is ***tawarruq***, a three-party structure used to generate cash. The
client buys a commodity from the bank on deferred payment at a markup, then sells it
immediately to a third party for cash. Two sales, both formally valid; net effect, cash now
against a larger sum later. It is *bay' al-'ina* with an extra party inserted to cure the
objection that the same two people are on both sides.

El-Gamal calls it one of the most egregious results of rent-seeking sharia arbitrage in Islamic
finance. More significantly, the **OIC Islamic Fiqh Academy** agreed. At its 19th session at
Sharjah, April 26 to 30, 2009, it resolved that organized *tawarruq* is not permissible,
reasoning that the simultaneous transactions between the financier and the *mustawriq* in
exchange for a financial obligation amount to a contrivance for obtaining cash and contain the
element of riba. The Academy distinguished classical from bank-structured *tawarruq*, permitting
the former.

Organized *tawarruq* nonetheless remains in widespread use, particularly for liquidity
management and personal finance, because no comparably liquid compliant alternative exists.
That gap between the transnational juristic ruling and industry practice is the sharpest
unresolved tension in the field.

## 4. Pakistan, 2002 to 2027

Pakistan's litigation resumed and has not finished.

After the 1999 judgment the government obtained a one-year extension, then filed a review
petition, and in June 2002 a newly constituted Shariat Appellate Bench remanded both earlier
judgments to the Federal Shariat Court. The case then sat for two decades.

The Federal Shariat Court decided it again on **April 28, 2022**, holding once more that all
types of interest on loans and debts — commercial, production-related, and personal alike —
fall within riba. It directed the government to complete the legislative amendments needed to
replace the affected laws by Dec. 31, 2022, and to eliminate interest from the economy by the
end of 2027.

The State Bank of Pakistan and several banks appealed to the Shariat Appellate Bench of the
Supreme Court, and the matter remains under review there. Pakistan is therefore in a position
with no parallel — a binding judicial deadline to remove interest from a functioning economy,
under appeal, with roughly a year remaining as of this writing.

# Part Ten: Islamic Banking in Operation

The preceding parts follow the prohibition and its enforcement. This part and the three after
it describe the industry that arose to comply with it, as the industry itself presents and
defends it, before Part Nine's critics have their full say. Islamic banking is a financial
system based strictly on the principles of Sharia, which views money as a tool to measure
value rather than an asset that can generate profit on its own.

## 1. The Three Golden Rules

To understand how these banks operate, one must first understand what they are legally and
ethically prohibited from doing:

1. **The ban on interest (*riba*).** Money cannot be loaned out to make more money. Charging
   or paying interest is completely forbidden. Part Three covers the scriptural basis; Part
   Four covers what the jurists made of it
2. **The ban on uncertainty (*gharar*).** Contracts must be fully transparent. Speculative
   side-bets — the derivatives and short selling described in Part One — are banned because
   they rely on excessive chance or hidden conditions
3. **The ban on harmful industries (*haram*).** Banks cannot finance businesses involved in
   alcohol, gambling, pork, pornography, weapons, or traditional interest-based financial
   firms

## 2. How Islamic Banks Make Money

Instead of lending money and charging interest, Islamic banks act as trading partners or
investors. They use specialized, asset-backed contracts to facilitate standard financial
needs:

- ***Murabaha* — cost-plus profit.** In a traditional mortgage, a bank lends money and is
  repaid with interest. In Islamic banking, the bank buys the physical property directly,
  then resells it to the customer at a higher price, paid in installments. The profit margin
  is tied to a tangible commercial trade transaction, not a penalty on the time value of
  money
- ***Mudaraba* — profit sharing.** The bank provides all of the capital and the entrepreneur
  provides the labor; profits are split at a pre-agreed ratio. *Musharaka* goes further: both
  parties chip in capital and share the risks, so if the business fails they both take a
  financial loss proportional to their investment
- ***Sukuk* — replacing bonds.** Conventional bonds pay investors regular interest. Sukuk
  grant investors partial ownership of a real-world asset — a factory or a skyscraper — and
  the payouts are a share of the actual revenues or rent generated by that asset, not a fixed
  interest payment

## 3. Islamic vs. Traditional Banking at a Glance

| Feature | Traditional Banking | Islamic Banking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core mechanism | Charging interest on money loans | Buying and selling tangible assets or sharing profits |
| Risk allocation | The borrower bears almost all risk | The bank and customer share risk together |
| Asset backing | Loans can be purely digital or unbacked | Every contract must link to a real physical asset |
| Penalties | Late payments trigger compounding interest fees | Late fees are restricted or given to charity |

## 4. Worked Examples: House, Car, and Sukuk

Because money cannot be loaned for interest, the bank must act as buyer, seller, landlord, or
partner in a real-world trade transaction. Three worked examples:

**Buying a house: the co-ownership model (*diminishing musharaka*).** Instead of a
traditional mortgage, the customer enters a joint-venture partnership with the bank:

- The scenario: a $400,000 home with a 20 percent down payment ($80,000). Customer and bank
  buy the house together — the customer holds 20 percent of the equity, the bank the
  remaining 80 percent ($320,000)
- Because the bank owns 80 percent, the occupier pays rent on the bank's share. Each month a
  single payment consists of two parts: rent for the bank's portion, and an equity payment
  buying a small percentage of the bank's shares
- Month by month the customer's ownership grows and the bank's shrinks; because the bank owns
  less of the house each month, the rent portion declines proportionally until the customer
  owns 100 percent of the property

**Buying a car: the cost-plus model (*murabaha*).** The bank uses a deferred-sale contract:

- The bank buys a $30,000 car outright from the dealership and holds the title
- It immediately resells the car to the customer at a fixed, higher price — say $35,000. The
  $5,000 markup is the bank's transparent profit for facilitating the trade
- The customer pays the $35,000 in equal monthly installments over five years. The total
  price is locked from day one: if a payment is missed, the bank cannot charge compounding
  penalties, because the price of a traded good cannot change after the contract is signed

**"Bonds": asset-backed certificates (*sukuk*).** A city wants to build a $100 million
airport terminal:

- Instead of issuing interest-bearing debt, the city issues sukuk certificates. Investors
  pool their money to fund construction, and each certificate represents direct fractional
  ownership of the physical terminal
- Once the terminal opens, real revenues — airlines leasing gates, passengers paying fees —
  are distributed to sukuk holders as rental yield or profit share. At maturity the city buys
  back the fractional shares at face value. The payouts are backed by the economic
  performance of a real asset, not a synthetic interest rate

**How equity builds.** In both a traditional 30-year fixed mortgage and a diminishing
musharaka contract, the buyer's goal is to go from a small down payment to full ownership. For
a $400,000 home with 10 percent down, the buyer starts at 10 percent equity either way. In the
traditional mortgage, early payments go overwhelmingly to interest, so equity grows slowly at
first and accelerates in the second half of the term. In diminishing musharaka the buyout rate
is typically structured linearly, so equity grows at a more consistent pace from day one. By
year 15 the musharaka model often shows higher actual equity for the consumer, whose early
payments in the conventional case have gone to unrecoverable interest. By year 30 both
pathways yield the same result — 100 percent ownership. The difference is the ethical and
legal structure used to get there: traditional financing rents money, whose price can
compound, fluctuate, or penalize; Islamic financing rents or buys a physical asset, with
profit tied to real commerce, rent, or trade margins.

## 5. Comparing the Home Contracts

The most common criticism of Islamic finance is that if the total cost is similar, an Islamic
home loan is just an interest-bearing loan wrapped in different vocabulary. On the surface, a
15-year fixed-rate mortgage and a 15-year diminishing musharaka look nearly identical, and the
monthly out-of-pocket payment may match to the penny. The difference is what is being paid
for:

- **Traditional mortgage.** You are borrowing money; interest is the price of borrowing it
  over time. In the eyes of the law the bank is a lender and you are a debtor. The bank has
  no interest in the home itself, only in getting its cash back
- **Islamic home loan.** You are entering a partnership to buy a physical asset. The bank's
  profit comes from rent on the portion of the house it owns plus the buyout of its shares.
  The bank is co-owner and landlord, not just a lender

| Feature | Traditional 15-Year Fixed Mortgage | Islamic Home Loan (Diminishing Musharaka) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal relationship | Lender and borrower; no interest in the home itself | Co-owners and partners in a joint venture |
| Source of profit | Interest (*riba*) charged on a cash loan | Rental income (*ijara*) on the bank's share of the property |
| Late fees | Compounding interest penalties, kept by the bank as profit | Flat administrative fees; punitive late fees must be donated to charity |
| Responsibility for disaster | The borrower bears 100 percent of the risk; the full loan remains owed | Shared risk; an uninsurable loss is split by ownership percentages |

## 6. What Happens If You Default

The truest test that these are different instruments is what happens when the buyer can no
longer pay.

**Default on a traditional mortgage:**

1. **Late fee accumulation.** Missed payments trigger late fees, and because interest
   compounds, the total debt grows larger every month payments are missed
2. **Foreclosure.** The bank uses the home as collateral, seizes it, and auctions it to
   recover its money
3. **The debt follows you.** If a crashed market leaves a $400,000 home selling for $300,000
   against $350,000 owed, the bank can sue for the $50,000 deficiency. The borrower loses the
   house and still owes money

**Default on an Islamic home loan:**

1. **No compounding debt.** Because interest is forbidden, missed payments cannot trigger
   compounding penalties. The obligation is capped at the agreed value of the shares; the
   bank cannot profit from the default
2. **Partnership dissolution.** Default triggers dissolution of the partnership, and the
   house is sold on the open market
3. **Equitable asset split.** Proceeds are divided by equity. If the house sells for $400,000
   and the customer had bought 40 percent of the shares before defaulting, the customer is
   entitled to 40 percent of the proceeds ($160,000), minus missed rent obligations. The bank
   cannot wipe out the customer's equity to cover a monetary debt

Traditional banking treats money as an asset that grows over time, so the borrower carries
almost all the risk. Islamic banking obliges the bank to assume the risks of property
ownership alongside the customer. The monthly payments are engineered to compete with
conventional rates, but the consumer protections built into the contract differ
fundamentally.

# Part Eleven: Why Islamic Banking Remains Niche

If Islamic banking is so favorable to the borrower, the question follows, why is it not more
popular? Islamic finance offers structural protections and shared risk, yet it remains a
niche alternative outside Muslim-majority hubs. The global Islamic finance market sits at
roughly $6 trillion; conventional global banking exceeds $150 trillion. The reasons come down
to cost, regulatory mismatch, fragmentation, and liquidity.

## 1. It Is Usually More Expensive for the Consumer

Because Islamic banks cannot rely on simple, automated digital debt, their operations are far
more intensive:

- **Compliance overhead.** Every transaction requires auditing by an independent panel of
  Sharia scholars
- **Asset overhead.** In a murabaha vehicle transaction, the bank must physically buy,
  temporarily hold, and legally resell the vehicle — extra legal steps, documentation, and
  administration
- **The bottom line.** To cover these costs, Islamic banks build higher margins into their
  rent or markups. The rules may favor the buyer, but the actual price is often higher than a
  conventional bank's interest rate

## 2. The Global Regulatory Framework Mismatch

The world's financial system was built over centuries to regulate money lending, not joint
asset ownership. When an Islamic bank operates in a Western country it faces friction:

- **The double-taxation trap.** Many jurisdictions levy a deed transfer tax each time a
  property changes hands. Because an Islamic bank legally buys the home and then transfers or
  shares ownership with the customer, the transaction can be taxed twice — once when the bank
  buys, again when the customer buys out the bank — unless the jurisdiction rewrites its tax
  code to allow otherwise
- **The bank capital problem.** Global banking regulations such as the Basel Accords set
  capital requirements by risk. These frameworks treat cash lending as standard banking but
  view co-owning real estate or businesses as volatile commercial investing, forcing Islamic
  banks to hold far larger reserves and making it harder to scale

## 3. Lack of Standardization

Islamic finance suffers from severe jurisdictional fragmentation. There is no single global
body deciding what is halal or haram in finance. A product celebrated as compliant in
Malaysia may be rejected as too close to standard interest by stricter scholars in Saudi
Arabia — the *illa* dispute of Part Four operating at industrial scale. This makes uniform,
mass-market international products very difficult to roll out.

## 4. Limited Liquidity Infrastructure

When a conventional bank runs short of cash for new mortgages, it borrows overnight from
other banks or the central bank at a standardized interest rate. Islamic banks cannot do
this, because interest-bearing borrowing is forbidden. Their alternatives — trading physical
commodities, issuing sukuk — form a much smaller and less flexible overnight market, which
severely limits how fast an Islamic bank can grow its pool of money. Islamic banking
effectively exchanges financial risk for operational complexity: protecting the buyer from
compounding penalties creates a structure that requires double the legal work, strict
religious oversight, and complex asset transfers.

## 5. Why Muslim-Majority Countries Stay Dual

The modern Islamic banking industry is itself a 20th-century invention — Part Eight's story.
No historical empire or caliphate relied on an "Islamic bank," because the bank did not exist
until the late Renaissance. Historical Muslim economies ran on Sharia-compliant commercial
law and decentralized trust networks: *mudaraba* merchant partnerships of the kind described
in Part Five; the *suftaja* bill of exchange, by which a merchant in Baghdad could deposit
cash locally and redeem paper for cash in Cairo or Istanbul — a direct inspiration for the
modern check; and the Ottoman cash waqf, the charitable endowment pool of Part Five, whose
murabaha-structured micro-loans funded schools and hospitals.

Today almost every Muslim-majority nation operates a dual-banking system, with interest-based
banks alongside Islamic ones, and no major country is fully Islamic in its financial
operations. The reasons:

1. **The legacy of colonialism.** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century European powers
   dismantled the historical trade networks of the Middle East and South Asia and embedded
   Western, interest-based legal and financial structures into their governance. By
   independence in the mid-1900s, the economic foundations, legal systems, and central banks
   of these countries were hardwired into the Western model
2. **The trap of global financial integration.** Participating in global trade means
   participating in an international financial grid — the U.S. dollar reserve system, the
   SWIFT network, the IMF — that runs on interest-bearing debt mechanics. A country that
   completely outlawed interest would isolate itself from it
3. **Domestic secular vs. religious jurisdictions.** Many large Muslim-majority countries —
   Egypt, Turkey, Indonesia — have secular constitutions or legal systems and maintain
   traditional banking deliberately, for compatibility with non-Muslim trade partners and
   diverse domestic corporate interests
4. **The "form over substance" criticism.** Even within the Islamic sectors of Saudi Arabia,
   Iran, or Malaysia, much of the public and many scholars criticize the industry for
   behaving too much like standard banking — a domestic echo of the sharia arbitrage charge
   in Part Nine. Many Islamic accounts peg their profit margins to Western benchmarks such as
   SOFR, and products that feel like interest-bearing loans in Arabic legal terminology
   inspire everyday consumers to choose conventional banks for convenience

# Part Twelve: Islamic Banking in the United States

For a reader of this document in the United States, the practical question is what is actually
available. There are no standalone, fully independent Islamic retail banks in the U.S.
Instead, Sharia-compliant banking is delivered through dedicated Islamic financial
institutions that partner with conventional banks, or through specialized "Islamic windows"
— faith-based divisions inside established FDIC-insured conventional banks. Because these
programs partner with federally regulated institutions, deposits are FDIC-insured up to
$250,000, exactly as at standard banks.

## 1. How Sharia Banking Reaches American Customers

The structural pattern mirrors Part Thirteen's fintech model in an older form: the Sharia-compliant
institution supplies the compliant contract structure and (usually) an independent Shariah
supervisory board, while the conventional partner supplies the charter, the balance sheet, and
the deposit insurance. The result is that no U.S. customer's legal counterparty is an Islamic
bank as such; it is a conventional bank operating a ring-fenced compliant program.

## 2. Nationwide Providers

- **UIF Corporation.** The most established, exclusively Shariah-compliant Islamic financial
  platform in the U.S. Partnered with University Bank, UIF offers nationwide *mudarabah*
  profit-sharing savings and time deposit accounts, has absorbed the legacy platform LARIBA,
  and provides halal home, vehicle, and commercial financing across all 50 states
- **Stearns Bank (Salaam Banking).** A nationwide, nationally chartered conventional bank
  operating a fully ring-fenced Salaam Banking program, with funds entirely segregated from
  the bank's traditional interest-bearing business. Supervised by an independent Shariah
  Supervisory Board under AAOIFI standards; offers business and nonprofit checking, savings
  accounts, and commercial real estate and equipment financing
- **Bank of Whittier.** An OCC-supervised national bank operating on a strict riba-free (RF)
  model across its entire book, with FDIC-insured checking, savings, and retirement/CD
  accounts available in all 50 states, using a declining participation in usufruct structure
  backed by annual independent audits

## 3. Regional and Special-Use Providers

- **Devon Bank.** A Chicago community bank with a deep faith-based commercial suite, offering
  *murabaha* and *ijara* residential home mortgages across roughly 34 states
- **Guidance Residential.** Not a day-to-day retail checking bank, but the largest dedicated
  Sharia-compliant home financing provider in the U.S., operating an independent
  *musharakah* co-ownership model across 34-plus states
- **Jafari No-Interest Credit Union.** An NCUA-insured credit union operating a 0 percent
  interest framework; its physical availability and specific financing accounts are primarily
  focused in Texas

## 4. Digital and Fintech Innovations

Several halal digital fintech apps — Wahed and Zoya among them — operate in the U.S. market.
They are not licensed banks themselves; they partner with FDIC-insured banks to provide
Sharia-compliant investment portfolios and automated cash management accounts. They are
described in detail in Part Thirteen.

# Part Thirteen: Islamic Fintech

Islamic financial technology apps restructure the back-end mechanics of banking to route
around rigid legacy banking codes. By operating as a technology layer rather than a licensed
bank, fintechs can dodge the structural hurdles — double taxation on property transfers and
steep commercial capital requirements — that historically choked out brick-and-mortar
Islamic banks.

## 1. Bypassing the Banking License: The BaaS Loophole

The most significant bypass is refusing to become a bank at all:

- **The problem.** A banking charter forces an institution to abide by laws written for
  interest-bearing cash lending, and regulators penalize banks that hold physical,
  non-monetary assets on their books
- **The fintech fix.** Startups use an embedded finance or Banking-as-a-Service (BaaS) model.
  The app is a front-end interface; the consumer cash sits with a fully licensed,
  FDIC-insured conventional partner bank
- **The compliance trick.** Software APIs "ring-fence" user funds: the app instructs the
  partner bank to route the money strictly into zero-interest accounts, isolating it from the
  conventional bank's interest-generating loan pools

## 2. Streamlining Asset Verification via Smart Contracts

The administrative overhead of tracking physical assets for murabaha or ijara drives up
operational costs. Automated smart contracts orchestrate the multi-step ownership transaction
— purchase from supplier, immediate deferred resale to consumer — in milliseconds, replacing
manual legal steps and letting fintechs offer cheaper rates than traditional firms.

## 3. Automated Sharia Screening and Purification

Staying compliant in investing once required expensive human analysts to audit corporate
balance sheets:

- **The problem.** Companies change their debt ratios constantly. A stock that was halal in
  January might carry too much interest-bearing debt by June
- **The fintech fix.** Platforms like Zoya and Wahed run automated data scrapers over
  thousands of global stocks daily. If interest-based debt spikes past permissible
  thresholds, the algorithm alerts the investor or flags the asset
- **Purification automation.** If a halal company earns a tiny fraction of income from an
  impermissible source — a tech company earning minor interest on its cash reserves — the
  software calculates the exact fraction the user must donate to charity to purify their
  investment yield

## 4. Overcoming Fragmented Religious Laws with Open Disclosures

Jurisdictional fragmentation — the standing problem traced from the *illa* dispute in Part
Four through Part Eleven — is addressed by radical transparency. Instead of trying to satisfy
every global scholar, apps publish their screening methodologies, transaction ledgers, and
the names of their Sharia Supervisory Boards openly. Users can inspect the ruleset themselves,
letting a platform build trust globally without waiting for a unified international standard.

## 5. The Reality Check

Technology solves user experience and administrative cost, not law. Digital apps cannot alter
a state's property tax code, and for complex home-equity co-ownership, fintechs must still
partner with licensed regional legal entities to avoid the double-taxation trap of Part
Eleven.

# Part Fourteen: Money Printing and Systemic Risk

The final movement of this document turns from contract law to monetary sovereignty. The
questions are why a government would print money, what that actually does, and what a total
collapse of the financial system would look like — the tail risks against which any financial
system, Islamic or conventional, has to be judged.

When governments "print money," they usually are not running printing presses. Modern central
banks create digital money to expand the nation's money supply. Doing this is highly risky,
yet countries resort to it to finance deficits, stimulate stagnant economies, or survive
catastrophic shocks.

## 1. Why a Country Prints More Money

- **To fund government deficits (debt monetization).** When a government spends more than it
  collects in taxes, it must borrow. If it cannot find lenders, the central bank can create
  new money to buy government bonds — effectively printing cash to pay public bills, military
  expenditures, or civil service wages
- **To stimulate a stagnant economy.** In a deep recession the velocity of money slows;
  businesses stop investing and consumers stop spending. Central banks expand the money
  supply to make borrowing cheap, aiming to push cash into the open market and boost activity
- **To prevent deflationary spirals.** If prices drop consistently, consumers delay purchases
  expecting cheaper goods later, paralyzing businesses. Central banks inject capital to
  engineer mild inflation and keep money moving

## 2. The Mechanics: Cheap Borrowing and Inflation

**Why printing makes borrowing cheap.** When a central bank injects new cash into the
commercial banking system, banks are flooded with excess reserves. The price of cash — the
interest rate — drops. Commercial banks do not earn by letting cash sit in a vault, so to
attract borrowers they lower rates on mortgages, car loans, and business credit.

**Why printing causes inflation.** Money is not wealth; it is a claim on real-world goods and
services. If the government prints a trillion units and hands them out, people have more cash
to spend, but the economy has not produced more houses, cars, or food. More cash chasing the
same quantity of goods produces a bidding war; sellers raise prices, and the purchasing power
of each unit falls.

## 3. The Benefits

- **An immediate financial lifeline.** It prevents a government from defaulting on short-term
  obligations or collapsing during unexpected crises
- **An economic jumpstart.** Injected cash lowers interest rates, giving businesses cheap
  capital for factories, equipment, and payroll
- **A safety valve for liquidity.** During the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, central banks
  expanded the digital money supply through quantitative easing, injecting trillions into
  frozen banking sectors and preventing a total collapse of the global financial system

## 4. The Issues

- **Severe price inflation.** Doubling the money in circulation without doubling the goods
  produced means more money chasing the same goods; prices rise across the board and real
  purchasing power falls
- **The destruction of savings.** Inflation punishes savers; if the cost of living doubles,
  a fixed bank balance is effectively cut in half
- **Loss of confidence and capital flight.** If citizens and investors believe the central
  bank will print to solve its problems, they dump the local currency for foreign assets such
  as dollars or hard assets such as gold, sending the currency into a downward spiral

## 5. Case Studies: Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Argentina

**Zimbabwe (mid-2000s).** Facing economic collapse and heavy debts after chaotic agricultural
disruptions, the government printed astronomical quantities of Zimbabwean dollars. Hyperinflation
peaked at an estimated 79.6 billion percent a month in late 2008; the central bank issued
100-trillion-dollar banknotes that could not buy a loaf of bread, and the currency was
abandoned for the U.S. dollar.

**Venezuela (2010s).** After a sharp drop in oil prices, the government under Nicolás Maduro
printed continuously to fund public services and balance the books. Stores stopped using
price tags because prices changed by the hour; the government repeatedly cut zeros from the
currency. By 2018 annual inflation had passed 1,000,000 percent, a chicken cost roughly 14
million bolivars, and barter and illegal dollar use became widespread.

**Argentina (2010s-2026).** Chronic deficits were covered for decades by printing pesos. By
late 2023 annual inflation breached 200 percent and the bank issued 10,000 and 20,000-peso
bills so citizens did not have to carry bricks of cash to buy groceries. After a drastic
political shift, the government cut spending, balanced the federal budget, and halted deficit
monetization; by mid-2026 monthly inflation had collapsed from 25.5 percent to 1.7 percent.
The emergency cuts raised short-term poverty, but the freeze on printing stabilized the peso
and began reining in a multi-decade inflation epidemic.

## 6. What a Total Systemic Collapse Looks Like

A total collapse of the global financial system means the breakdown of the infrastructure
that handles global trade, credit, and currency exchange. The cascade runs: systemic trigger,
then credit freeze, then supply chain halt, then social breakdown.

- **The credit freeze.** Banks stop lending entirely because they no longer trust that anyone
  can repay
- **The ATM blackout.** If the digital banking grid fails, accounts are unreachable; ATMs
  empty within hours and cards stop processing
- **The supply chain halt.** Modern shipping depends on financial letters of credit. If
  international settlement stops, cargo ships stay docked, trucks stop moving, and grocery
  stores run out of food within days

## 7. Why Collapse Is Catastrophic — and What Survives It

Modern life depends on a continuous, uninterrupted flow of transaction data:

- **Mass unemployment.** Without credit, even profitable businesses cannot meet wages or buy
  raw materials; layoffs number in the millions immediately
- **Erosion of life savings.** Paper money, stock portfolios, and pensions can lose their
  value, wiping out generations of family savings
- **Geopolitical and social unrest.** When people cannot buy food, fuel, or medicine, law and
  order degrades quickly

The silver lining is the concept of **creative destruction**, from economist Joseph
Schumpeter. Systemic crises offer rare opportunities to fix broken structural incentives:

- **Cleansing zombie entities.** Decades of cheap borrowing keep inefficient, debt-ridden
  companies alive without producing real value; a collapse clears them out, moving capital
  and workers toward productive sectors
- **Elimination of bad debt.** A crash works as a reset button — unpayable corporate,
  sovereign, and personal debts are erased, letting the economy rebuild on a cleaner
  foundation
- **Systemic reform.** Catastrophes force regulatory rebuilds. The 1929 Great Depression
  produced FDIC deposit insurance, and future resets could open the way to decentralized or
  asset-backed alternatives — including, notably, the Sharia-compliant models described in
  Parts Ten through Thirteen

# Part Fifteen: A Hypothetically Islamic Macroeconomy

If the prohibitions traced in this document governed the world entirely, what would happen to
the monetary powers just described? The answer is hypothetical — no state has ever run a
fully Islamic macroeconomy, for the reasons Part Eleven gives — but the structure of the
system permits a clear answer.

## 1. Could Governments Print Money to Avoid Default?

Not in the way they do today. The mechanic of a government borrowing a mountain of cash and
then printing new digital money to service the interest and principal is structurally blocked:

- **No standard sovereign bonds.** Governments would not issue interest-bearing bonds to fund
  deficits. They would issue sukuk — asset-backed certificates linked to real public
  infrastructure such as highways, ports, or power grids
- **Debt cannot outgrow the economy.** Because money must be anchored to physical assets, a
  government cannot issue $10 trillion in debt against $2 trillion in real public assets
- **Printing to bail out is banned.** A failed project triggers a profit-and-loss-sharing
  resolution. A central bank is forbidden from printing unbacked currency to rescue a failed
  financial structure or erase a deficit; doing so is a human distortion of the currency's
  value — *gharar* and *riba* at sovereign scale

## 2. Would Hyperinflation and Collapse Still Be Possible?

Yes, but the root causes would change. The system eliminates "financial-error"
hyperinflation — printing money to cover bad debts, as in Zimbabwe or Venezuela. It remains
vulnerable to real-economy shocks:

- **Natural disasters and scarcity.** A massive drought destroying grain supplies, or a war
destroying energy grids, collapses the supply of real goods. Prices skyrocket because the
scarcity is real — no printing press required — and a real-economy collapse remains possible
- **Asset bubbles.** Speculation is banned, but markets can still collectively overvalue a
tangible asset class such as real estate or commodities. If those prices collapse, the banks
co-owning the assets take real losses and a recession follows
- **Imported inflation.** A country relying on imports from a hyperinflating non-Islamic
economy imports that inflation into domestic prices

## 3. How Things Would Be Different

| Macroeconomic Factor | The Conventional World | The Hypothetical Islamic World |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deficit spending | Practically unlimited; governments borrow and let future generations or inflation pay it off | Strictly capped by the volume of tangible, income-generating public assets |
| The velocity of money | Wealth can sit idle in interest-bearing accounts or derivatives | Hoarding is penalized by mandatory charity (*zakat*), keeping wealth in circulation |
| Systemic crises | Violent and sudden; unbacked credit expansions blow asset bubbles that pop overnight (as in 2008) | Slow and real-asset driven; tied to crop yields, manufacturing limits, or infrastructure failures |

An Islamic financial world would likely be a more stable, slower-growing, and
inflation-resistant economy. It would eliminate the catastrophic risks of speculative banking,
synthetic derivatives, and the printing press used as a political escape hatch. It would pay
for that stability with tighter limits on rapid government spending and a strict vulnerability
to real-world resource scarcity. Whether that trade is worth making is precisely the form-versus-substance
question that Part Seventeen leaves open. The fiscal machinery behind the zakat
row of the table above — the levy itself, the hoarding prohibitions, and their modern
administration — is the subject of Part Sixteen.

# Part Sixteen: Taxation and Hoarding in Islamic Law

The macroeconomy of Part Fifteen rests on fiscal instruments this document has described only
in passing. Where a modern state taxes transactions, payrolls, and profits, the classical
system settled on a small number of broad levies on wealth, land, and protected residency, and
paired them with a legal attack on hoarding that operates at the level of accumulation, of
market conduct, and of price. This part sets out the instruments, the hoarding prohibitions
that give the levies their purpose, and the modern legislative record. The political narrative
under Umar, from which most of the enforcement episodes come, is treated in the companion
document *Umar ibn al-Khattab*.

## 1. The Fiscal Instruments

**Zakat** is the only fiscal obligation the Quran states as a pillar of the religion, and it
is a tax on stock rather than flow — 2.5 percent a year on wealth that has remained above the
*nisab* threshold, conventionally 85 grams of gold or 595 grams of silver in value, for one
lunar year. Four features carry the analysis:

- **The threshold shields the poor.** Only surplus wealth above the nisab is zakatable, so the
  levy touches accumulated capital rather than subsistence
- **It taxes idleness, not activity.** Because the levy recurs annually on whatever wealth
  remains, wealth must be deployed — invested, lent as *mudaraba*, spent — or it bleeds 2.5
  percent a year. Section 6 returns to what this does
- **The state collects it.** Quran 9:103 addresses the Prophet directly — take from their
  wealth a charity by which you purify them — and Abu Bakr's campaigns against the tribes that
  refused to hand zakat to the state after the Prophet's death (632-33) are the historical
  evidence that it was understood as a compulsory public levy rather than private charity
- **Spending is earmarked.** Quran 9:60 fixes eight recipient categories: the poor, the needy,
  the collectors, those whose hearts are to be reconciled, captives, debtors, the cause of
  God, and the stranded traveler. It is the rare tax legally bound to specific ends

**Ushr and kharaj** are the land taxes. Agricultural output paid *ushr*, the tenth: 10 percent
on rain-fed land and 5 percent where the state built the irrigation, a two-rate structure that
prices the state's contribution into the levy. Conquered land paid *kharaj*, which remained
due even when its owners converted to Islam — one of the era's sharpest fiscal controversies.
Abu Yusuf's *Kitab al-Kharaj*, written in the late eighth century at the request of Harun
al-Rashid, is the classic treatment, and it reports Umar's decision for the Sawad of Iraq —
tax the land in perpetuity rather than confiscate it — as the founding precedent. The logic
is that the tax falls on the one base that cannot relocate.

**Jizya** is the annual levy on non-Muslim free adult males in exchange for state protection
and exemption from military service; its payers owed no zakat. The classical rates are
graduated by ability to pay: Abu Yusuf transmits Umar's schedule of 48 dirhams for the
wealthy, 24 for the middle class, and 12 for laborers and craftsmen, and the standard fiqh
exemptions sweep broadly — women, children, the elderly, monks, the disabled, the destitute.
Two enforcement norms recorded under Umar define what the tax was understood to be:

- **The welfare clause.** Umar found an elderly Jewish man begging in Medina to pay his jizya
  and feed himself, said "we have not done him justice if we consumed his youth and then
  abandoned him in his old age," waived the tax permanently, and put the man on a Bayt al-Mal
  pension
- **Refund on failure to protect.** When Abu Ubaidah's army could no longer defend Homs before
  Yarmouk, the year's jizya was returned in full

A protection fee, refundable when the protection fails, is a different instrument in kind from
a general revenue tax — closer to an insurance premium than to a levy on activity.

## 2. Against the Modern Tax Framework

Set against the instruments of conventional public finance:

| Dimension | Modern income and sales taxes | Tariffs | The Islamic instruments |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base | Income, payroll, consumption — flows | Imports — a targeted flow | Idle wealth, land, protected residency — stocks and status |
| Deadweight loss | Yes: the wedge prices some marginal trades out of existence | Yes, doubled by retaliation | Minimal: no transaction is taxed, so no trade is priced out |
| Who bears it | Split between buyer and seller by elasticity | Consumers, diffusely | Only those above the threshold, by published schedule |
| Price distortion | Shifts supply and demand | Raises import prices and shelters domestic margins | None: prices carry no levy |
| Deficit finance | Standard: bonds and, in the limit, monetization | None | Structurally absent — no interest-bearing debt (Part Fifteen) |

One quasi-tariff existed: *ushr* charged on imported goods at border markets, roughly a 10
percent customs levy on foreign merchants, while Muslim traders paid zakat on their trading
stock instead. It was never used as industrial policy. Where a modern state protects a sector
behind a price wall, the Islamic instruments partner capital with enterprise — the
*musharaka* and *mudaraba* of Part Five — so that support for an industry takes the form of
risk-sharing rather than a tax on the consumer.

## 3. Kanz: Hoarded Wealth and the Unpaid Zakat

The core text is Quran 9:34-35, addressed to those who "hoard gold and silver and do not spend
it in the way of God": a painful punishment is announced for them, and on the Last Day the
hoards will be heated and brandished. Quran 3:180 extends the image — what the miserly
withhold will be hung around their necks — and 104:1-3 condemns the one who collects wealth
and counts it, thinking it will make him immortal.

The legal meaning is fixed by the reports rather than the bare text. A Bedouin asked Ibn Umar
about the passage, and Ibn Umar answered — Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Zakat — that the *kanz*
is wealth on which zakat has not been paid; a report in the Muwatta transmits the same rule
from Umar. The consequence is precise: accumulation is not itself criminalized — a Muslim may
be wealthy — but wealth that sits idle and unpaid of zakat is the condemned thing. Paying
zakat purifies the hoard; refusing the tax constitutes it. The hoarding prohibition and the
zakat are the same subject seen from two sides, which is why the punishment texts sit in the
zakat passages of the Quran.

## 4. Ihtikar: The Market Hoarder

The second layer is hoarding as market conduct — buying up a necessity to withhold it and
force the price up. The report in Sahih Muslim and the Muwatta gives the Prophet's words:
none hoards but the sinner, *la yahtikir illa khati'*. The jurists built the prohibition with
three edges:

- **The object must be a necessity**, staple food above all. Hoarding non-essential goods is
  disapproved at most
- **The harm is the withholding, not the owning** — cornering supply in a condition of
  scarcity so that people are compelled to buy at whatever price is set
- **Ordinary inventory is lawful.** A merchant's stock, a household's stored grain, a trader
  who buys in season and sells later at a fair margin: all permitted. The line, in
  al-Qaradawi's standard modern statement in *Fiqh al-Zakah*, is between supplying the market
  across time and withholding from it

And the state's remedy is characteristically constrained. When prices rose in Medina, the
Prophet declined to impose ceilings — the report across the sunan collections has him say that
God is the holder of the price and that he would not force sellers — while forcing
transparency instead: the report of him dipping his hand into a grain pile, finding it damp
beneath, and ordering the seller to expose it. The system polices conduct and leaves prices
free: the manipulation is forbidden, the signal is not jammed.

## 5. Umar and the Famine of 638

The Year of the Drought, *'Am al-Ramada*, is the enforcement test case. The outline is in the
companion document: famine, grain shipped from Egypt, a caliph eating bread and olive oil
until his people were fed. The hoarding chapter completes it. When holders withheld grain from
the market during the scarcity, Umar ruled that stores of needed food could be taken for
distribution, rebuking those who held back while the community went hungry, and he and his
governors policed the markets for hoarding throughout the famine — while never legislating a
maximum price. The reports of the episode vary in wording across the adab and fiqh
collections, and the usual caution applies; what is consistent is the practice. Emergency
requisition of withheld necessities, yes; administered prices, no.

## 6. The Structural Cure and the Modern Record

| Layer | Instrument | The test | The sanction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accumulation | Zakat on idle wealth | Is the wealth zakat-paid and circulating? | The annual levy; the Quranic condemnation if unpaid |
| Market conduct | The *ihtikar* prohibition | Is a necessity being cornered in scarcity? | Prohibition; requisition at need |
| Prices | Non-intervention | — | Prices stay free; transparency is forced instead |

A wealth tax that recurs on whatever sits still gives every holder of capital a standing
incentive to move it — into trade, into partnership, into charity. This is the mechanism
behind Part Fifteen's table, which contrasts the conventional world's trillions in stagnant
interest-bearing accounts with an Islamic world in which hoarding cash is penalized by
mandatory charity. The *ihtikar* prohibition supplies the micro-level conduct rule and zakat
the macro-level pressure, and because the levy falls on stock rather than transactions, the
anti-hoarding incentive arrives with little of the deadweight loss that a transaction tax
produces — the trade-off of section 2 inverted in the system's favor.

The modern states that legislated the system:

- **Pakistan** — the Zakat and Ushr Ordinance of 1980 made zakat a deducted-at-source levy of
  2.5 percent on financial assets, with an exemption nisab and self-declaration for personal
  wealth, and ushr a levy on agricultural produce; the 18th Amendment of 2010 devolved
  administration to the provinces
- **Sudan** — enacted a first zakat law in April 1980 and consolidated it in 1984, with a
  national Zakat Bureau, the fullest attempt to run zakat as a state revenue instrument
- **Malaysia** — zakat is administered at state level by religious councils, several
  corporatized into professional collection bodies
- **Saudi Arabia** — the tax authority collects zakat at 2.5 percent on the profits of
  Saudi-owned companies, a corporate zakat in place of corporate income tax for national
  owners

The pattern is the one Part Nine predicts. Where zakat is state-collected, the debate is
whether a fixed-rate wealth levy can carry a modern budget, and almost everywhere conventional
taxes were layered on top — which returns the subject to the form-versus-substance question
Part Seventeen leaves open.

# Part Seventeen: Comparisons and the Analytical Question

## 1. The Three Traditions Side by Side

| | Judaism | Christianity | Islam |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scriptural basis | Exodus 22:25, Leviticus 25:36-37, Deuteronomy 23:19-20 | Inherited from the Torah, developed conciliar and scholastic | Quran 30:39, 4:161, 3:130, 2:275-280, plus the six-commodities hadith |
| Scope as written | Prohibited toward fellow Israelites, permitted toward foreigners | Universal, no exemption | Universal among believers, with no insider-outsider distinction stated |
| Extension beyond lending | None | None | Yes — *riba al-fadl* covers unequal spot barter of like commodities |
| Peak severity | Prohibition with communal enforcement | Denial of sacraments and Christian burial, from 1179 | Quranic warning of war from God and His Messenger |
| Principal authorized workaround | *Heter iska*, restructuring a loan as joint investment | *Montes pietatis* cost recovery, from 1515 | *Mudaraba* and *murabaha* as genuine alternatives; *bay' al-'ina* and *tawarruq* as contested ones |
| Status of the ban today | Operative within observant practice via *heter iska* | Formally withdrawn; penalties abolished 1830 | Formally operative, institutionally enforced, contested in application |

Two patterns emerge. Every one of the three produced a device that restructures a loan into a
partnership or a sale, and the *heter iska*, the *montes*, and *tawarruq* are recognizably the
same solution to the same problem. And Islam is the only one of the three in which the
prohibition remains legally live at scale, which is the fact requiring explanation.

## 2. Calder's Explanation

**Ryan Calder's** "God's Technicians: Religious Jurists and the Usury Ban in Judaism,
Christianity, and Islam," in the *European Journal of Sociology* (2016), is the best available
answer, and it locates the cause in institutions rather than in doctrine.

His thesis is that the fate of a religious interest ban tracks the fate of religious jurists.
Where jurists are absent, no systematic ban emerges. Where they establish themselves as
technical experts whose authoritative reasoning is required to interpret complex financial
arrangements, the ban appears and thrives. Where they lose institutional standing or their
traditional social role comes under attack, the ban withers.

Applied to the three cases, the account is economical. The Christian ban collapsed as the
scholastic jurists lost their monopoly on evaluating commercial arrangements, and Part Six's
sequence from 1515 to 1830 is what that collapse looked like. The Jewish ban survived inside a
community whose jurists retained authority over observant practice, but without a state to
enforce it. And the Islamic ban is live today because Islamic jurists, far from disappearing,
reconstituted themselves in the 20th century as certified technical experts inside a modern
financial industry — which is exactly what a Sharia Supervisory Board is.

Calder's framework also explains El-Gamal's complaint without needing to adjudicate it. If the
ban survives because jurists function as technical experts, then a system that generates
demand for their certification is the ban's condition of survival rather than a corruption of
it. El-Gamal's indulgences comparison and Calder's analysis are describing the same mechanism
and evaluating it oppositely.

## 3. Where the Positions Divide

Most of the record is shared. All sides accept that the Quran categorically prohibits riba
without defining it, that the hadith extended the prohibition to unequal barter, that the
schools disagreed on the operative cause, that workarounds existed throughout, and that the
Ottoman religious establishment authorized interest-bearing cash waqfs. The disagreements are
of four kinds.

- **Form or substance.** Does Islamic law regulate the structure of a transaction or its
  economic effect? Every dispute in this document reduces to this — *bay' al-'ina* in the 11th
  century, cash waqfs in the 16th, *tawarruq* in the 21st. The formalist answer makes the law
  administrable and makes it gameable. The substantive answer makes it principled and makes it
  indeterminate, since a judgment about economic effect is a judgment, not a rule
- **What the prohibition is for.** If the ground is preventing exploitation, the modernists are
  right that a commercial deposit is outside it, and *riba al-fadl* becomes inexplicable. If the
  ground is excluding return that does not arise from real activity and risk, *riba al-fadl*
  makes sense and bank interest falls squarely inside. The Quran supplies material for both, in
  2:279's *neither wronging nor being wronged* and in 275's categorical separation of trade from
  interest
- **Whether consensus settles it.** The 1985 Fiqh Academy resolution and the Pakistani
  judgments treat the matter as closed by classical agreement. The Tantawi fatwa and the
  Ottoman precedent show that the agreement was never as complete as the claim requires — and
  the *illa* dispute of Part Four shows that scriptural indeterminacy was resolved by
  reasoning, not by revelation
- **Symmetrical difficulty.** The traditionalist has to explain why an industry built on the
  prohibition reproduces conventional finance at higher cost, and has to live with the Fiqh
  Academy's own ruling against a structure the industry cannot function without. The modernist
  has to explain 2:275, which anticipates and rejects the trade-interest analogy, and has to
  explain *riba al-fadl*, which no theory of exploitation reaches
- **Gambling, *gharar*, and the purpose test.** Part One set out the finance-professional
  defense of derivatives and leverage — risk transfer from those who bear it to those who
  price it, liquidity, price discovery — against the casino charge. That defense is, in
  substance, the purposive reading: a wager on nothing serves no economic function, while a
  hedge does. The traditionalist camp agrees with the conclusion but rejects the method,
  because the jurists' formal categories — *gharar* bans, the requirement that gain arise
  from real activity — were built precisely so that compliance would not depend on a case-by-case
  judgment of economic purpose. The modern financial system is therefore the strongest
  evidence each side holds: it supplies the examples of genuinely productive risk transfer the
  purposive reader needs, and equally the speculative excesses the formalist needs, since
  nothing in the formal test stopped leveraged side-betting from growing to systemic size

**The usable rule.** Match the claim to the source. The Quran establishes that riba is
forbidden and not what it is. The hadith establishes the categories. The *fiqh* literature
establishes what jurists held and how they differed. Institutional fatwas establish current
authority, not settled truth, and they conflict. Economic history establishes what people
actually did, which is that credit was priced throughout. And nothing in any of these settles
whether a given modern instrument is lawful, which is why the question is still open after
fourteen centuries.
