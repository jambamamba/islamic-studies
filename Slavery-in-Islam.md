# Slavery in Islam: A Chronological History

> **About this document.** A chronological account of slavery in Islamic history, from the
> pre-Islamic Near East to the present. Part One establishes what slavery was before Islam,
> including in Jewish and Christian law. Part Two examines what the Quran and the Prophet's
> practice did to the institution — the honest answer is regulation and restriction rather
> than abolition — and covers the enslaved and freed people of the Prophet's household.
> Parts Three through Eight follow the institution forward through the Rashidun caliphate,
> the juristic settlement, the Umayyads and Abbasids, the regional systems from the Mamluks
> to the Ottomans to Morocco, the 19th-century expansion, and legal abolition. Part Nine
> covers the contemporary situation. Part Ten compares the Islamic systems with Jewish,
> Christian, and Atlantic slavery on scale, character, and abolition. Part Eleven maps the
> modern historiographical debate. Researched Aug. 22, 2026, from academic and confessional
> secondary literature; primary Arabic texts were not consulted directly, and claims are
> attributed to the scholars and studies that make them. Where a widely repeated story is
> of contested historicity, that is stated rather than glossed.

# Part One: Before Islam

## 1. The Ancient Near East and the Mediterranean

Slavery was universal in the world Islam entered. It is documented in Sumer, Babylon, Assyria,
Egypt, Israel, Persia, Greece, Rome, Byzantium, and the Sasanian empire, and no ancient legal
system abolished it. Pagans, Jews, Christians, and later Muslims bought and sold human beings
for millennia across the same territory — the point Bernard Lewis makes at the opening of
*Race and Slavery in the Middle East*, and it frames everything that follows. The historical
question is never whether a tradition invented slavery. It is what each did with an
institution it inherited.

Two features of the pre-Islamic Near East matter for the chapters ahead. Enslavement had many
sources: war captivity, birth, debt, judicial sentence, kidnapping, sale of self or children,
and abandonment. And status was gradated rather than binary, with debt bondage, clientage,
temple servitude, and later serfdom occupying the ground between free and owned.

## 2. Jewish and Christian Law

Both traditions regulated slavery and neither abolished it, which is the honest baseline for
any comparison in Part Ten.

**Hebrew scripture** regulated an institution that long predated it. The Mosaic law
distinguished Hebrew from foreign slaves, limited Hebrew indenture to six years with release
in the seventh, and granted protections without parallel in surrounding legal systems —
rights to food and clothing, to keep a wife, and not to be sold to foreigners. The
distinction between the treatment owed to Israelites and to foreigners is a structural
feature, not an incidental one.

**Christianity** produced no scriptural abolition. The New Testament tells masters to treat
slaves justly and slaves to obey their masters, and Paul returns Onesimus to Philemon.
Ecclesiastical practice followed suit for over a millennium. Most consequentially for the
period covered in Part Six, Pope Nicholas V issued *Dum Diversas* on June 18, 1452,
authorizing Alfonso V of Portugal to reduce Saracens, pagans, and other unbelievers to
perpetual slavery, and *Romanus Pontifex* in 1455 extended it. Those bulls supplied the moral
license for the Portuguese trade in West Africa. Any account that treats Christian abolition
as the natural expression of Christian scripture has to explain the 1,400 years in between.

## 3. Pre-Islamic Arabia

Slavery was established and unremarkable in the Arabia of the *Jahiliyya*, roughly the 5th to
7th centuries CE. The primary source was captives from intertribal raiding and warfare,
supplemented by debt bondage, hereditary status, kidnapping, sale of self or of children,
judicial enslavement, and sacrificial enslavement. Enslaved men were *'abd*, women *ama*. They
performed domestic labor, herded livestock, and served as concubines. Owners held broad
rights over their persons, including sexual access, and — a practice the Quran addresses
directly — enslaved women were forced into prostitution for their owners' profit.

Mecca in the Prophet's lifetime held a slave population drawn from Ethiopia, Persia, the
Byzantine world, and the Arabian interior. The best-known individual case is **Bilal ibn
Rabah**, owned by Umayya ibn Khalaf, one of the wealthiest men of Quraysh, who had him
staked out in the desert sun with a rock on his chest for refusing to renounce Islam. Abu
Bakr purchased and freed him. Bilal was not the Prophet's slave, a point routinely garbled in
popular accounts.

One caution on sources. Nearly everything known about pre-Islamic Arabia comes through
material written down from the late 8th century onward and reshaped continuously thereafter,
and the *Jahiliyya* functions in that literature partly as a moral foil for Islam. The
picture above is what the sources report, not what excavation confirms.

# Part Two: The Quran and the Prophet, 610-632

## 1. What the Quran Says

The Quran neither institutes slavery nor abolishes it. It assumes the institution and
legislates within it, and the legislation moves in one direction throughout: toward release.

**Manumission as virtue, expiation, and public finance.** The Quran treats freeing a slave as
among the highest acts of righteousness (2:177), makes it one of the eight legitimate
categories of *zakat* expenditure — *wa fi al-riqab*, "and for the freeing of necks" (9:60) —
and prescribes it as expiation for specific wrongs: accidental homicide (4:92), a broken oath
(5:89), and a repudiated *zihar* divorce (58:3). Surah al-Balad frames the ascent to virtue as
*fakku raqaba*, the untying of a neck (90:12-13).

**The self-purchase right.** Verse 24:33 instructs owners, where an enslaved person seeks a
contract of release and shows good in them, to grant it and to give them from their own
wealth. This is the scriptural basis of *mukataba*, the written self-purchase contract of
Part Four.

**A prohibition on forced prostitution.** The same verse forbids compelling enslaved women
into prostitution, addressing the Arabian practice named in Part One.

**Captives.** Verse 47:4 instructs that after a battle is decided, captives be bound and then
either freed as a favor or ransomed. Read on its own, it does not authorize enslaving them —
which is why it is central to the reformist argument that Quranic slavery was meant to
terminate rather than persist.

**The phrase that carries the weight.** *Ma malakat aymanukum*, "what your right hands
possess," appears at 4:3, 4:24, 4:25, 16:71, 23:5-6, 24:33, 24:58, and 33:50. Classical
jurists read it as denoting slaves and, in the relevant contexts, as licensing sexual access
without a marriage contract. A minority modern reading construes it as those under one's
contractual protection or dependency. The classical reading is the one the entire juristic
tradition built on; the modern reading has to explain how every school got it wrong.

**Hadith on treatment.** The best-known report is *muttafaq 'alayh*, in both Bukhari (30) and
Muslim (1661). Abu Dharr had insulted a man's mother; the Prophet told him he retained traces
of pre-Islamic ignorance, and that "they are your brothers and your assistants. Allah has
placed them in your hands. Whoever has his brother in his custody, let him feed him with the
same food he eats and clothe him with the same clothes he wears" — adding that they must not
be given tasks beyond their capacity, and that whoever assigns such a task must help with it.
The narrator Ma'rur ibn Suwayd reports meeting Abu Dharr afterward wearing the same clothes
as his servant.

## 2. The Closing of the Channels

The single most defensible claim for Islamic reform of slavery is structural rather than
moral. The tradition narrowed enslavement to two channels and closed the rest.

| Channel | Pre-Islamic Arabia | Classical Islamic law |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Captivity in war | Permitted | Permitted — the sole new source |
| Birth to an enslaved mother | Permitted | Permitted, unless the owner is the father |
| Debt | Permitted | Closed |
| Kidnapping and abduction | Permitted | Closed |
| Sale of self or of one's children | Permitted | Closed |
| Judicial sentence | Permitted | Closed |
| Enslaving a free Muslim | — | Prohibited |
| Enslaving a *dhimmi* under Muslim rule | — | Prohibited |

This is a real restriction and it had real effects: it is why Islamic slave systems depended
on a permanent frontier supplying captives from outside, and why the trans-Saharan, Caucasian,
and East African trades of Parts Five through Seven took the shape they did. It is also the
restriction most often breached in practice. Almost every abuse catalogued later in this
document — the kidnapping that fed the Saharan caravans, the enslavement of Muslims in West
Africa, the Circassian trade in Muslim children — was illegal by the law being invoked to
justify it.

## 3. The Prophet's Slaves and Freedmen

The Prophet owned slaves, freed slaves, received them as gifts, and distributed them. The
sources name a substantial household of *mawali* — freed clients whose relationship to him
outlasted their servitude.

Popular lists circulating online conflate three different statuses, so the distinctions matter.

- **Enslaved and freed by him.** **Zayd ibn Harithah** is the central case. Taken captive as
  a child from the Banu Kalb and sold at Mecca, he came into the Prophet's ownership, was
  freed, and was then adopted as his son. He was among the earliest converts, commanded
  expeditions, and is the only Companion named in the Quran (33:37). **Thawban** was bought
  and freed and chose to remain in the household. **Abu Rafi' al-Qibti** was given to the
  Prophet by his uncle Abbas and freed, and described himself thereafter as the Prophet's
  *mawla*. **Safina**, originally Mahran, was freed by the Prophet or by Umm Salama
- **Enslaved women of the household.** **Mariya al-Qibtiyya**, a Copt, was sent as a gift
  from the Byzantine governor of Egypt around 628 together with her sister Sirin. She bore the
  Prophet's son Ibrahim, who died in infancy — which under the later law of Part Four made
  her an *umm al-walad*. **Rayhana bint Zayd** came from the Banu Qurayza. **Salma**, Umm
  Rafi', served the household, alongside others named in the biographical literature
- **Free servants, commonly miscounted.** **Anas ibn Malik** appears on many online lists of
  the Prophet's slaves. He was a free boy of Medina whose mother sent him to serve the
  household, and the conflation of *khadim* (servant) with *'abd* (slave) is the most frequent
  error in this material. **Bilal**, as noted, belonged to Umayya ibn Khalaf and was freed by
  Abu Bakr

Two things are true at once and both belong in an honest account. The Prophet's *mawali*
became figures of standing in the community, and Zayd's trajectory from captive child to
adopted son and battlefield commander is not a trivial fact about the social meaning of
servile status in early Islam. And the Prophet did not free everyone, did not declare slavery
unlawful, and distributed captives after campaigns. The tradition itself does not dispute
either half.

## 4. Concubinage

This is the hardest material in the document and the place where sanitizing it would be a
disservice.

Classical jurisprudence across all four Sunni schools, and in Shia law, held that a male
owner could have sexual relations with a woman he owned without a marriage contract, on the
authority of *ma malakat aymanukum* and of 4:24 concerning captives. No marriage, no dower,
and no consent requirement attached. The children of such a union were freeborn and legitimate,
took the father's lineage, and their mother became an *umm al-walad*.

**Kecia Ali's argument** in *Sexual Ethics and Islam* (2006) and *Marriage and Slavery in
Early Islam* (2010) is the most important scholarly intervention here, and it cuts deeper
than a complaint about concubinage. Ali shows that the classical jurists conceptualized
marriage itself using the vocabulary of ownership drawn from slavery — the dower understood
as the price of sexual access — and that the concubinage model therefore shaped the entire
juristic treatment of marriage. Her conclusion is that consent never became a central category
of classical Islamic sexual ethics, because the paradigm within which the jurists worked did
not require it. That is a claim about the architecture of the law, not about a marginal
practice, and it is why the concubinage question cannot be quarantined.

**Jonathan Brown's** treatment in *Slavery and Islam* (2019) attracted the sharpest criticism
of that book for the suggestion that becoming a concubine was not necessarily a bad outcome
for an enslaved woman. Reviewers, including Bernard Freamon, judged this close to a "happy
slave" argument and unsupportable. The criticism is well taken, and the underlying difficulty
is not one any position in Part Eleven escapes: a legal category that made sexual access an
incident of ownership cannot be reconciled with consent-based ethics by pointing to cases
where the outcome was materially comfortable.

## 5. The Verdict on Part Two

Setting apologetic and polemical framings aside, the record supports a narrow set of claims.

- The Quran restricted enslavement to war captivity and birth, and closed every other channel
  the pre-Islamic Arabs had used
- It made manumission a virtue, an expiation, a *zakat* category, and — through 24:33 — an
  enforceable claim an enslaved person could initiate
- It required humane treatment, and the Prophetic tradition on that is explicit and strong
- It did not abolish slavery, did not set a terminal date, and licensed sexual access to
  enslaved women without marriage or consent
- Any claim that Islam abolished slavery in principle is a modern theological construction,
  not a historical description. Any claim that Islam introduced or invented slavery is
  equally false

# Part Three: The Rashidun and the Conquests, 632-661

## 1. Umar ibn al-Khattab, Jerusalem, and the Camel

The best-known story about slavery and early Islamic authority concerns Umar's journey to
receive the surrender of Jerusalem in 638.

**The story.** Umar traveled from Medina with a single servant and one camel rather than an
imperial retinue. The two took turns riding, and in some versions divided the journey in
thirds — Umar riding one third, the servant riding one third, and both walking the final
third to spare the animal. When they approached the city, it was the servant's turn on the
camel. Umar refused to displace him. So the caliph of an empire entered Jerusalem on foot
leading a camel ridden by his servant, to the astonishment of the assembled Muslim commanders
and of the patriarch Sophronius.

**What it is worth.** As a statement of a political ideal, a great deal. The story is one of
the most widely transmitted images in Islamic political ethics, and its point is precise: the
servile relation confers no dignity differential before God, and a ruler who forgets that has
forfeited the office's warrant. It is the narrative counterpart to the Abu Dharr hadith in
Part Two.

**As history, it needs qualification.** The Jerusalem meeting is not reported in any early
source and appears first in a mid-8th-century chronicle focused on the encounter between Umar
and Sophronius, as Robert Hoyland notes. Al-Tabari's account of the Jerusalem settlement
derives from Sayf ibn Umar, who wrote roughly 140 years after the events and whom Ibn Hajar
graded weak in hadith while excellent in history. The camel detail belongs to the edifying
biographical tradition rather than to documented record, and it is worth noticing what it
demonstrates either way: a community that circulated this story as its ideal of just rule was
a community in which slaveholding was nonetheless normal at every level, including the
caliph's own household.

## 2. Umar's Legal Interventions

Umar's documented contributions to the law of slavery are more consequential than the camel.

The rule that became *umm al-walad* is attributed to him: "if a slave-girl gives birth to a
child by her master, he must not sell her." That single ruling removed a category of women
from the market permanently and guaranteed their release at the owner's death, and it became
settled doctrine in all schools. He is also reported to have prohibited the enslavement of
Arabs, an ethnic restriction with no Quranic basis that nonetheless shaped who could be
lawfully enslaved for centuries.

## 3. Conquest Changes the Arithmetic

The conquests of the 630s to 750s transformed the scale of the institution. A restriction
limiting enslavement to war captivity is a modest constraint in a small polity with occasional
raids; it is close to unlimited licence for a state in permanent expansion across Syria, Iraq,
Egypt, Persia, North Africa, Spain, and Central Asia. Captives arrived in numbers no Arabian
system had contemplated.

The consequence was the *mawali* class — freed non-Arab clients — which within two generations
supplied a disproportionate share of Islamic scholarship, including major figures in hadith,
law, and Quranic exegesis. That is a genuine feature of the system: servile origin was not a
permanent caste bar, and the Yaqeen Institute's paper on the *mawali* documents the scholarly
contribution in detail. It is also not an argument for the institution. A system that produces
distinguished freedmen is still a system that produced them by enslaving their parents.

# Part Four: The Juristic Settlement, 8th-10th Centuries

Between roughly 750 and 950 the jurists converted the Quranic material and the Prophetic
reports into a detailed body of law. Every later system in this document operated inside that
framework, invoked it, or breached it while claiming it.

## 1. The Structure of the Law

The law of slavery (*riqq*) ran on a few organizing principles.

- **Two lawful origins only.** Captivity in a lawful war, and birth to an enslaved mother
  whose owner is not the father. Everything else was unlawful acquisition, whatever the market
  did in practice
- **Protected categories.** Free Muslims could not be enslaved. *Dhimmis* — non-Muslims
  living under Muslim rule with protected status — could not be enslaved. Conversion after
  enslavement did not, however, produce automatic release, which is the doctrinal gap that
  made the trans-Saharan and East African trades sustainable for a millennium
- **Legal personhood, partial.** An enslaved person was property for purposes of sale,
  inheritance, and pledge, and a legal person for purposes of religious obligation,
  contractual capacity in defined areas, marriage with the owner's permission, and criminal
  liability. Injury to them was actionable; killing them was homicide
- **Gradated *awrah* and status.** As set out in the companion document on hijab, the covering
  obligation itself differed for enslaved and free women, which is one index of how thoroughly
  legal status permeated the system
- **Castration prohibited.** The law forbade it without exception, and the prohibition was
  never repealed. Part Five covers what happened anyway

## 2. The Manumission Instruments

The law supplied five distinct routes out, which is unusually many by comparative standards
and is the strongest single point in the tradition's favor.

| Instrument | Arabic | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *'Itq* | عتق | Outright manumission, valid by simple declaration and treated as a major act of merit |
| *Mukataba* | مكاتبة | A written self-purchase contract, grounded in 24:33; the owner is instructed to grant it when sought, and to contribute from their own wealth |
| *Tadbir* | تدبير | Manumission taking effect at the owner's death, declared in advance and irrevocable |
| *Umm al-walad* | أم الولد | A woman who bears her owner's acknowledged child cannot be sold or gifted, and is freed at his death; her child is freeborn |
| *Kaffara* | كفارة | Compulsory manumission as expiation for accidental homicide, a broken oath, or *zihar* |

Add the *zakat* category of *fi al-riqab*, which made emancipation a charge on public
religious finance rather than a matter of private conscience alone.

## 3. What the Law Achieved, and What It Licensed

Both columns need stating, because the historiographical dispute in Part Eleven is largely
about which one to emphasize.

**What it achieved.** A slave system with a legally guaranteed exit, an enforceable
self-purchase right, a rule making the children of owner-concubine unions freeborn heirs, a
prohibition on the most common historical sources of enslavement, and a strong ethical
literature on treatment. The *umm al-walad* rule in particular is structurally significant:
it meant that in elite households the reproductive pathway led out of slavery rather than
deeper into it within a single generation, which is the reverse of the Atlantic pattern
described in Part Ten. Al-Halimi's *Minhaj fi shu'ab al-iman*, the subject of a Yaqeen
Institute study, is a representative statement of the ethical framework as the tradition
understood it.

**What it licensed.** Ownership of human beings in perpetuity; hereditary enslavement through
the maternal line; sexual access without marriage or consent; sale, gift, pledge, and
inheritance of persons; and a permanent structural demand for captives from beyond the
frontier that made continuous slave-raiding an economic necessity rather than an incidental
byproduct. And the law contained no terminal provision. Nothing in it pointed to a date after
which the institution would cease.

That last point is the crux of the entire modern debate. A body of law that regulates
generously but sets no end can be read as an abolitionist trajectory interrupted by human
failure, or as a stable equilibrium the tradition had no internal reason to leave. Part Eleven
lays out who reads it which way.

# Part Five: Umayyad and Abbasid, 661-1258

## 1. Scale and Supply

Under the Umayyads and Abbasids slavery became an imperial system with organized long-distance
supply. Three corridors developed and they operated, with interruptions, for over a millennium.

- **Trans-Saharan**, from West and Central Africa north across the desert to the Maghrib,
  Egypt, and the Middle East
- **Red Sea and East African**, from the Horn and the Swahili coast to Arabia, the Persian
  Gulf, and India
- **Eurasian**, from the Caucasus, the Turkic steppe, and Slavic Europe — the *saqaliba* — into
  the caliphal heartlands

Aggregate estimates are wide and the scholarship is candid about why: decentralized routes,
many intermediaries, and thin documentation. Paul Lovejoy's figures, the most cited, give
roughly 7,420,000 for trans-Saharan exports between 650 and 1900, and between about 3,255,700
and 4,038,200 for Red Sea and East African exports between 800 and 1896. The consensus reached
by Lovejoy, John Hunwick, and Ralph Austen places the combined total between 10 and 17 million
people removed from sub-Saharan Africa from roughly 650 CE to the early 20th century —
comparable to the Atlantic trade at minimum and probably exceeding it once the full duration
is counted. Lovejoy himself cautions that these are midpoints of ranges extending appreciably
in both directions.

Slavery in these societies was predominantly domestic, military, administrative, and
artisanal rather than agricultural, which is the most important structural difference from the
Atlantic system. The exceptions are the exceptions that matter, and the largest of them
produced the next section.

## 2. The Zanj Rebellion, 869-883

Basran landowners imported several thousand East Africans — the *Zanj* — into southern Iraq to
drain the salt marshes east of Basra. The labor was brutal, the subsistence minimal, and the
arrangement was plantation slavery in all but name, inside the Abbasid heartland.

In September 869 Ali ibn Muhammad, a Persian claiming descent from Ali and Fatima, organized
work crews of between 500 and 5,000 men by naming the injustice of their condition and
promising freedom and wealth. The rebellion lasted fourteen years, captured Basra, and cost
tens of thousands of lives before the Abbasids suppressed it in 883. Historians rank it among
the most destructive uprisings in Abbasid history.

The Zanj revolt is the single most important corrective to the claim that Islamic slavery was
uniformly mild and domestic. Where the economics favored large-scale agricultural exploitation,
Muslim slaveholders organized it, and the enslaved responded as enslaved people have responded
to plantation regimes everywhere. Many captured rebels were then absorbed into military
slavery.

## 3. Military Slavery

The most distinctive Islamic institution in this field has no real parallel elsewhere:
slavery as a route to the summit of state power.

Caliph al-Mu'tasim (r. 833-842) systematically recruited Turkic slave soldiers — *ghilman*, or
*ghulams* — from beyond the caliphate's frontiers, seeking a force loyal to the ruler
personally rather than to Arab tribal or Persian factional interests. The logic was that a man
severed from kin and homeland had no constituency but his master. It worked, and then it
worked too well: within a generation the *ghilman* commanders were making and unmaking caliphs.

The institution spread across the Islamic world and became the normal basis of military power
in it. Its logical terminus is Part Six.

## 4. Eunuchs

Castration was categorically prohibited in Islamic law, and the prohibition was never waived.
It was also comprehensively circumvented. The demand came from the harem system, which
required attendants with access to secluded women, and from court administration, where
eunuchs — *khadim* in the sources — served as guardians, intermediaries, and household
managers with unusual freedom of movement and proximity to power.

The circumvention was geographic. Boys were castrated outside Muslim territory, or on its
frontiers where enforcement was weak, and imported already mutilated. Castration houses
operated at Verdun and in Venice, producing eunuchs for export to Egypt and North Africa. The
mortality of the procedure was very high.

This is the clearest case in the document of a pattern worth naming explicitly: a legal
prohibition satisfied by relocating the prohibited act beyond the jurisdiction, while the
demand that drove it sat at the center of Muslim political life. Lewis emphasizes it, and it
is difficult to answer.

# Part Six: The Regional Systems, 1250-1800

## 1. The Mamluk Sultanate

In 1250 the slave soldiers took the throne outright. The Mamluks — a self-perpetuating
military elite of slaves and freedmen, principally Turkic and later Circassian — displaced the
Ayyubids and ruled Egypt and Syria until the Ottoman conquest in 1517. Boys were purchased
from the Central Asian steppe and the Caucasus, converted, trained, manumitted on completion
of training, and promoted on merit within the corps. The state is conventionally divided into
a Turkish period (1250-1382) and a Circassian one (1382-1517), the latter beginning when
Circassians recruited as slaves by the Turkish Mamluk sultans displaced their recruiters.

The Mamluk system defeated the Mongols at Ayn Jalut and ended the Crusader states. It is the
strongest evidence that servile status in Islamic societies could be a career track rather
than a terminal condition — and it is worth being precise about the limit of that point.
Mamluk mobility was available to a narrow cohort of imported boys selected for military
promise. It says nothing about the domestic, agricultural, and sexual slavery that constituted
the overwhelming majority of the institution.

## 2. The Delhi Sultanate and Mughal India

The same logic produced the same result in India. Military slaves — *ghulams* in Arabic,
*bandagan* in Persian — were valued precisely because they were natally alienated, and they
founded the Delhi Sultanate: the first dynasty (1206-1290) is known in Indian historiography
as the Slave Dynasty. The sultans imported Turks to build a corps of mounted archers.

Slavery in medieval India also ran far beyond the military. Enslavement came through warfare,
famine, poverty, and abduction, organized markets developed in Delhi and other cities, and
imports arrived from Africa, West Asia, and Central Asia. **Firuz Shah Tughlaq** (r.
1351-1388) is the emblematic case of scale: he is reported to have held some 180,000 slaves,
including roughly 12,000 skilled artisans, with subordinate rulers and officials sending
slaves to court as tribute. Whether the Delhi Sultanate constituted a slave society or a
society with slaves is an active scholarly question.

## 3. The Ottoman Empire

The Ottoman system was the most elaborate ever built on Islamic legal foundations, and Ehud
Toledano's *Slavery and Abolition in the Ottoman Middle East* (1998) is the standard treatment.
Toledano's central observation reframes the whole subject: in the Ottoman polity many members
of the ruling elite were legally slaves of the sultan — *kul* — and could in principle be
ordered to surrender their labor, property, or lives at will. Slavery was simultaneously a
condition of subjection and the principal ladder of social mobility, conferring status and
political power through military, bureaucratic, and household patronage networks.

The system had distinct realms, and Toledano organizes his book around individuals
representing each.

- **The *kul* elite**, comprising the sultan's military and civilian servants, including grand
  viziers who governed the empire while remaining, in law, the sultan's property
- **The *devshirme***, the levy of Christian boys from the Balkans and Anatolia, converted and
  trained for the Janissary corps and the palace service — a systematic enslavement of subject
  children that sat awkwardly with the legal prohibition on enslaving *dhimmis*
- **The harem**, where enslaved women could rise to become mothers of sultans and the most
  powerful figures at court; the *umm al-walad* rule of Part Four is what made that pathway
  legally possible
- **Court and elite eunuchs**, supplied through the arrangements described in Part Five
- **Domestic slavery**, the numerically dominant form, and **Circassian agricultural slavery**
  in Anatolia after the Russian conquest of the Caucasus
- **Slave dealers**, whose commercial networks Toledano reconstructs from Ottoman archives

Toledano also notes how little studied all this was: Ottomanists and Islamicists largely
avoided the subject, and comparative scholarship neglected slave systems that were not
plantation-based.

## 4. Morocco and the Abid al-Bukhari

Sultan Moulay Ismail (r. 1672-1727) built a slave army of Black soldiers called the **'Abid
al-Bukhari** — "the slaves of al-Bukhari," sworn on the hadith collection. At its height it
numbered somewhere between 50,000 and 100,000 men and formed the military spine of the
Moroccan state. Men and women were gathered into camps, the men trained for war and crafts,
the women in domestic service before palace placement and marriage to the soldiers, producing
a self-reproducing military caste.

**Chouki El Hamel's** *Black Morocco: A History of Slavery, Race, and Islam* (Cambridge, 2013)
is the essential study, and its argument is pointed. Many of those conscripted were Black
Moroccan Muslims — free people whose enslavement was flatly unlawful under the rules in Part
Four. El Hamel's broader thesis is that Islamic ideology alone cannot explain social relations
in Northwest Africa, and that what operated there was racial slavery: distinct in form from
American chattel slavery but organized around blackness in a way the law denied. The Moroccan
case is the sharpest single refutation of the claim that Islamic slavery was never racialized.

## 5. The Barbary Corsairs

From the 16th to the 18th centuries corsairs based at Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Salé raided
Mediterranean shipping and coastal settlements, taking European captives for ransom, galley
labor, and domestic service. Raids reached Italy, Spain, France, Portugal, and as far north as
England, Ireland, and Iceland.

**Robert C. Davis**, in *Christian Slaves, Muslim Masters* (Palgrave, 2003), estimates that
between one million and 1.25 million Europeans were taken between 1500 and 1800 — figures well
above earlier estimates, and derived by extrapolation from replacement-rate reasoning rather
than from direct counts, which is where the methodological criticism of the book concentrates.
The order of magnitude is nonetheless not seriously contested.

Two points follow. This traffic ran concurrently with the Atlantic trade, so the 16th to 18th
centuries saw large-scale enslavement moving in both directions across the Mediterranean and
the Atlantic. And it complicates any account of slavery as a purely European crime against
non-Europeans, without in the least excusing the Atlantic system.

## 6. Ahmad Baba and the Race Question

The most important internal Islamic critique of slavery before the 19th century came from
Timbuktu. **Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti** (1556-1627) wrote *Mi'raj al-Su'ud* in 1615, drawing on a
century of Timbuktu jurisprudence, to attack the association of blackness with servitude and
specifically the racialized reading of the curse of Ham.

His argument was doctrinally exact: Islamic law grounds liability to enslavement in unbelief,
not in race or descent. No one is a slave by nature, and a Muslim of any ethnicity is immune.
Applied honestly, the principle rendered a substantial portion of the trans-Saharan trade as
then conducted illegal, because much of it moved Muslims.

The limitation is equally important and Ahmad Baba states it himself. Having severed
enslavement from race, he reattached it to religion, and then classified much of West Africa
as non-Muslim or only lightly Islamized — thereby licensing the enslavement of those
populations. He is the tradition's most rigorous critic of racial slavery and simultaneously a
theorist of religious slavery. That combination is characteristic of what internal critique
looked like before abolitionism existed as a category.

# Part Seven: The Nineteenth Century

## 1. Expansion, Not Decline

The century in which Europe abolished slavery was the century in which Islamic slavery reached
its peak volume. This is counterintuitive and it is the single most important fact in this part
of the chronology.

The drivers were economic and technological. Clove and coconut plantations on Zanzibar and
Pemba, date plantations in the Gulf, grain and textile production in West Africa, and pearl
diving in the Persian Gulf all created large-scale demand for agricultural and extractive
labor — the plantation pattern that earlier Islamic slavery had largely avoided. Firearms
reaching the African interior made raiding more efficient. And as the Atlantic trade was
suppressed after 1807, African supply networks redirected toward Saharan and Indian Ocean
markets. Lovejoy's figures show trans-Saharan exports running at roughly 12,000 a year through
the 19th century.

## 2. Sokoto

The Sokoto Caliphate, founded by Usman dan Fodio's jihad in 1804 across what is now northern
Nigeria, became the largest state in 19th-century sub-Saharan Africa and, on **Paul Lovejoy's**
assessment in *Slavery, Commerce and Production in the Sokoto Caliphate*, one of the largest
slave societies in recent history. Slavery was not incidental to it; it was the backbone of the
economy, organized in plantations (*rinji*) around the major cities, and Lovejoy's argument is
explicit that Islam was a major influence on the political, economic, and legal structures that
sustained it.

Sokoto matters for the argument because it is a case where slavery expanded under a state
founded on a program of Islamic revival and legal purification, not under a lapsed or
nominally Muslim regime. It also generated a genuine internal jurisprudence of enslavement's
limits: the jihad's own justification turned on the claim that its targets were unbelievers or
apostates, which made the lawfulness of enslaving them a live legal question rather than a
formality, and ransoming practices developed as a partial check.

## 3. Zanzibar and Oman

Said bin Sultan al-Busaidi moved the Omani court to Zanzibar in 1840, an index of how central
the East African trade had become to Omani state finances. Omani Arab rule built an economy on
clove and coconut plantations worked by people captured on the East African mainland, and the
19th-century expansion of those plantations drove a corresponding expansion of the slave
system. By some estimates enslaved people made up as much as two-thirds of the archipelago's
population at its peak. Zanzibar's slave market became the most notorious in the Indian Ocean
and the principal target of British naval and diplomatic pressure.

## 4. Ottoman Abolition Under Pressure

The Ottoman sequence is the best-documented case of abolition in a Muslim polity, and its
shape is instructive: a long series of decrees, most of them driven by British diplomatic
pressure and most of them poorly enforced.

| Year | Measure | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1830 | Firman of Mahmud II | Freedom for white slaves |
| 1847 | Closure of the Istanbul slave market | Largely cosmetic; moved the trade indoors and out of diplomatic view |
| 1857 | Firman prohibiting the import of African slaves | Issued under diplomatic pressure, not enforced; the trade continued in Tripoli into the 1890s |
| 1880 | Anglo-Ottoman Convention | Further trade suppression commitments |
| 1887 | Imperial firman | Chattel slavery declared no longer legally recognized; every person in the empire held to be free by law |
| 1889 | Kanunname of Abdul Hamid II, Dec. 30 | Law against the Black slave trade, again after British pressure |
| 1890 | Brussels Conference Act | Obliged the empire to free illegally imported slaves and permit foreign embassies to reclaim enslaved citizens; enforced from 1892 |
| 1908-1913 | Young Turk reforms | The 1908 constitution excluded slavery |
| 1926 | Turkish Civil Code, Feb. 17 | Modeled on the Swiss code; no legal recognition of slave status |
| 1933 | Turkey ratifies the 1926 League of Nations Slavery Convention | Formal completion |

Two observations. The Ottomans were, in the standard scholarly formulation, reluctantly drawn
into abolitionist policy, and found the prohibitions difficult and in some provinces
effectively impossible to enforce. And there was no Ottoman abolitionist movement in the
Anglo-American sense — no societies, no petitions, no press campaign. Recent work in *IJMES*
argues that what existed instead was a diffuse, daily practice of subversion and resistance by
many actors working against the institution locally, which is a different phenomenon and
should not be confused with organized abolitionism.

## 5. Islamic Abolitionism

An indigenous Islamic case against slavery did develop in the 19th century, and **Haroon
Bashir's** *Slavery, Abolition, and Islam* (Oxford) is the recent synthesis. Its practitioners
worked from inside the tradition rather than adopting European arguments wholesale.

- **Muhammad Abduh**, grand mufti of Egypt, argued that the Prophet personally opposed slavery
  and that the higher objectives of the law — *maqasid al-shari'a* — were incompatible with it,
  reading human dignity as a governing principle rather than a sentiment
- **Sayyid Ahmad Khan** in India developed a Quranic abolitionist argument, resting heavily on
  47:4 and on the reading that the Quranic trajectory pointed toward termination
- **Later rationalists** — Fazlur Rahman and Ghulam Ahmad Parwez in Pakistan — extended the
  argument, and Clarence-Smith identifies them as the sharpest opponents of what he calls the
  neo-slave ideology of the later 20th century

The structure of the argument is worth naming, because it recurs in every other reform debate
in Islam: it moves from the text's rules to the text's purposes, and treats the rules as
instruments the purposes may supersede. That is the same move that appears in the hijab debate,
and it draws the same objection.

## 6. Islamic Resistance to Abolition

Abolition was contested, and the resistance was religious as well as economic.
**Clarence-Smith's** *Islam and the Abolition of Slavery* (Oxford, 2006) is the first general
survey of the Islamic debate and its findings are unflattering to all sides. Islamic groups
had, in his assessment, an ambivalent and generally muted impact on the legal repudiation of
slavery — while a religious commitment to abolition was precisely what was needed if the new
legislation was to be enforced at all.

The core resistance argument was the one Lewis identifies: since slavery was not forbidden by
Islam it was lawful, and what is lawful is not open to amendment. Revisionist movements from
the 18th century onward split on this. Those insisting on the literal authority of the founding
texts found abandoning slavery difficult in principle, since the texts plainly regulate it.
Clarence-Smith's more interesting claim is that the sharia was less clear on the legality of
servitude than is usually assumed, and that progressive jurists working within the schools
might have reached full emancipation over the long run — a counterfactual, but a considered one.

# Part Eight: Legal Abolition, 1900-1981

Formal abolition in Muslim-majority states came late, largely through colonial administration,
treaty obligation, or state modernization rather than through religious reform, and in several
cases within living memory.

| Year | State | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1926 | Turkey | Civil Code; convention ratified 1933 |
| 1949 | Kuwait | |
| 1952 | Qatar | |
| 1962 | Saudi Arabia | Royal decree; the Hijaz had been a principal destination of the Red Sea trade |
| 1962 | Yemen | |
| 1970 | Oman | The state whose Zanzibar plantations drove the 19th-century East African trade |
| 1981 | Mauritania | The last country in the world to abolish slavery; criminalized only in 2007 |

Two things stand out. Slavery remained legal in the birthplace of Islam until 1962, which is
after the founding of the United Nations, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and the
independence of most of Africa. And abolition in Mauritania in 1981 was declaratory only: no
criminal penalty attached until 2007, which is the direct cause of the situation described
next.

# Part Nine: The Contemporary Situation

## 1. Mauritania

Mauritania is the clearest surviving case of descent-based chattel slavery anywhere.

The 2023 Global Slavery Index ranks Mauritania among the ten countries with the highest
prevalence of modern slavery, estimating 32.0 people per 1,000 — roughly 149,000 individuals —
much of it hereditary. People are born into servitude, treated as property, and held in forced
labor without pay or freedom of movement. The affected population is overwhelmingly
**Haratin**, descendants of Black Africans enslaved by Arab-Berber masters, who constitute
around 40 percent of the national population; anti-slavery organizations estimate hereditary
enslavement affects between 10 and 20 percent of the country.

Enforcement is the failure point. Slavery was abolished in 1981 and criminalized only in 2007,
prosecutions are rare, and the practical obstacles compound: survivors often lack alternative
skills or economic options, social stigma attaches to manumission and escape, and the practice
is culturally normalized in some communities. Legal abolition without enforcement produced a
country where the institution is illegal, open, and ongoing.

## 2. The Islamic State

In August 2014 the Islamic State overran the Sinjar region and enslaved approximately 7,000
Yazidi women and girls, forcing conversions, distributing captives among fighters, and
operating sale markets. Documents recovered after the fall of Mosul stated that Yazidi women
"are property, permissible for sale, ownership, and sexual relations by the mujahidin."

The justification was explicitly juristic, not merely opportunistic. The group's magazine
*Dabiq* ran the enslavement under the heading of "the revival of slavery before the Hour,"
invoking the classical category of *sabaya* — female captives of war — and analogizing the
Yazidis to polytheists whom the Companions were reported to have enslaved and sold. In other
words, the argument ran directly through the material in Parts Two and Four, and it did not
require fabricating anything: it required treating the classical law as operative and
classifying the Yazidis as non-monotheists outside *dhimmi* protection.

This is why Part Nine cannot be separated from Part Four, and why the doctrinal question below
is not academic. The classical rules were never repealed by any recognized authority. They
were abandoned in practice and declared obsolete by consensus of contemporary institutions, and
a group willing to reject that consensus found the legal machinery intact.

## 3. Libya, Trafficking, and the Kafala System

Three distinct contemporary phenomena get grouped under "slavery in the Muslim world," and
they are not the same thing.

- **Libya.** In November 2017 CNN filmed a slave auction near Tripoli, recording a dozen men
  sold for as little as $400, and reported markets at nine locations. The IOM had reported in
  April 2017 that sub-Saharan migrants transiting Libya toward Europe were being detained by
  smugglers and militias and sold. The UN Security Council condemned it in December 2017 and
  Libyan authorities opened an investigation. This is trafficking amid state collapse rather
  than a legal institution, but the auction format and the racial pattern of who was sold echo
  the Saharan trade with uncomfortable exactness
- **The kafala system.** Migrant labor sponsorship across the Gulf ties a worker's legal status
  to a single employer, and the resulting conditions — confiscated passports, unpaid wages,
  restricted movement, inability to change jobs — are documented by human rights organizations
  as producing forced labor at scale. This is not slavery in the legal sense and calling it
  that obscures the analysis, but the structural feature it shares with slavery is that a
  person's legal capacity is held by another private party
- **Global trafficking.** Modern slavery in the Walk Free sense — forced labor, forced
  marriage, debt bondage — is a worldwide phenomenon that is not distinctively Islamic, and
  the highest-prevalence countries include states of every religious composition

## 4. The Unresolved Doctrinal Question

No Muslim state permits slavery today, and every significant religious authority holds it
closed. The question the tradition has not settled is *why* it is closed, and the answer
determines whether it could reopen.

- **Abolition by circumstance.** The classical rules remain valid but their preconditions no
  longer obtain, because the lawful war captivity that alone generated new slaves is regulated
  out of existence by treaty and by the states' own commitments. On this view the rules are
  dormant, not void — and a group that repudiates the treaties, as in section 2, revives them
- **Abolition by consensus.** Contemporary *ijma'*, expressed through state ratification of
  international conventions and through the fatwa bodies, has closed the matter, and consensus
  binds. The difficulty is that a consensus reached under external pressure is exactly the kind
  traditionalists elsewhere treat as weak
- **Abolition by purpose.** The *maqasid* argument of Abduh and Ahmad Khan: freedom is the
  Quranic objective, the manumission provisions are its instruments, and slavery was always
  scheduled for termination. Jonathan Brown arrives near here, concluding that because God
  wishes freedom for people, "abolition is the best means to make that wish a universal
  reality. If doing so is within our means, Islam can give no reason this should not be done"
- **Abolition as external imposition, resisted.** The position Clarence-Smith documents among
  literalists and Lewis identifies as the diehard argument: what the texts permit cannot be
  forbidden. It has no institutional standing today, and it is the position the Islamic State
  operationalized

# Part Ten: Comparisons

## 1. Scale

Comparative figures need to be handled carefully, because the Atlantic numbers rest on a
voyage-by-voyage database and the Islamic numbers rest on estimation from fragmentary records.

| System | Estimated people enslaved and transported | Period | Evidentiary basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trans-Atlantic | c. 12.5 million embarked, c. 10.7 million surviving the Middle Passage | 1526-1866 | *Slave Voyages* database: 34,948 documented voyages, an estimated 66-80 percent of the total |
| Trans-Saharan | c. 7.4 million | 650-1900 | Lovejoy's estimates from caravan and market records |
| Red Sea and East African | c. 3.3-4.0 million | 800-1896 | Lovejoy; wide confidence intervals |
| Islamic total from sub-Saharan Africa | c. 10-17 million | c. 650-early 20th c. | Lovejoy, Hunwick, Austen consensus |
| Barbary captives from Europe | c. 1-1.25 million | 1500-1800 | Davis, by extrapolation; method contested |

The Atlantic trade moved more people in a shorter time — three and a half centuries against
thirteen — and its documentation is far better. The Islamic trades ran longer and, aggregated
over their full duration, probably moved a comparable or larger number. Neither figure is a
moral argument, and both are frequently deployed as one.

Two demographic points do carry analytical weight. Lewis observes that despite centuries of
African importation, few identifiable Black populations remain in much of the Middle East, and
attributes this to castration, high mortality in transit and in service, and the absorption of
descendants — whereas the Americas retain large populations descended from the enslaved. And
the reason for that difference is structural: Islamic slavery consumed people, through eunuch
production and low reproduction rates in domestic and military service, while Atlantic slavery
reproduced them as a labor commodity.

## 2. Judaism and Christianity

Set side by side, the three traditions differ in details and converge in structure.

| | Jewish law | Christianity | Islamic law |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scriptural abolition | No | No | No |
| Regulation of an inherited institution | Yes | Minimal in scripture | Extensive |
| Insider/outsider distinction | Hebrew versus foreign slaves | Notionally universal; in practice unbelievers enslavable, per *Dum Diversas* | Muslims and *dhimmis* protected; unbelievers enslavable |
| Term limits | Six years for Hebrew slaves | None | None, but five manumission instruments and a self-purchase right |
| Manumission as religious merit | Present | Present in practice | Central, and funded from *zakat* |
| Sexual access to enslaved women | Regulated | Condemned in principle, widespread in practice | Lawful, without marriage or consent |
| Status of the owner's children by an enslaved woman | Variable | Generally enslaved in the Americas | Freeborn, legitimate, and paternal-line heirs; mother becomes *umm al-walad* |
| Institutional abolition | Effectively via diaspora legal disuse | 19th century, contested from within | 20th century, largely externally driven |

The two rows that most distinguish Islamic law are the last-but-one and the manumission
instruments. The *umm al-walad* rule inverted the Atlantic pattern in which the offspring of
owner-slave unions augmented the owner's property. And no other premodern legal system gave
enslaved people an enforceable claim to purchase their own freedom.

The row that most damages the Islamic case is the sexual one, where the tradition is not merely
permissive in practice but affirmative in law.

## 3. What Differed from Atlantic Slavery, and What Did Not

**Real differences.**

- **Function.** Predominantly domestic, military, administrative, and artisanal, rather than
  agricultural-export. The exceptions — Zanj Iraq, Sokoto, Zanzibar — are real and large, but
  they are exceptions rather than the norm
- **Mobility.** Slavery could lead to the throne. The Mamluk sultans, the Delhi Slave Dynasty,
  Ottoman grand viziers, and Valide Sultans have no Atlantic counterpart of any kind
- **Race.** Islamic law grounded enslavability in unbelief, not descent, and Ahmad Baba argued
  the point explicitly in 1615. The Atlantic system was constitutively racial
- **Exit.** Manumission was frequent, religiously meritorious, publicly funded, and in the
  *mukataba* and *umm al-walad* cases legally claimable
- **Descent.** Freed status generally carried no permanent caste disability; the *mawali*
  produced leading scholars within two generations

**Where the distinction fails.**

- **Race in practice.** El Hamel's *Black Morocco* demonstrates racialized slavery organized
  around blackness in a Muslim society, including the unlawful enslavement of Black Muslims.
  Lewis documents color prejudice throughout the written and visual record. The law's
  colorblindness was not the practice's
- **Plantation brutality.** Where the economics favored it, Muslim slaveholders built plantation
  regimes, and Zanj, Sokoto, and Zanzibar are the proof
- **Eunuch production.** A category of atrocity with no Atlantic parallel, sustained for a
  millennium by outsourcing a prohibited act
- **Sexual exploitation.** Systematic in both, and legally sanctioned in one
- **Legal violation as norm.** The restrictions of Part Two were the system's chief moral
  claim, and they were breached continuously — Muslims enslaved, *dhimmi* children levied,
  free people kidnapped

## 4. Who Ended It

This is the comparison that most needs stating plainly, because both the apologetic and the
polemical accounts distort it.

Slavery was ended, as a global legal institution, by a movement that arose in Protestant
Britain and North America in the late 18th century, drew on Christian arguments, and was then
imposed worldwide through British naval power, treaty pressure, and colonial administration.
The sequence — Somerset in 1772, the British trade in 1807, British colonies in 1833, France in
1848, Russian serfdom in 1861, the United States in 1865, Brazil in 1888 — precedes every
abolition in Part Eight by decades or a century.

Three qualifications keep that from being a claim about religious superiority.

- Christian scripture and 1,400 years of Christian practice supported slavery, and *Dum
  Diversas* licensed the Atlantic trade. Abolition was a break with Christian tradition made in
  Christian language, not the expression of it
- The states that abolished slavery were the states that had industrialized the largest slave
  system in history, and were abolishing their own institution
- An indigenous Islamic abolitionism did exist, in Abduh, Ahmad Khan, and their successors, and
  Clarence-Smith's judgment is that its weakness mattered — not because Muslims needed Europeans
  to supply the moral argument, but because without religious commitment the new laws could not
  be enforced

# Part Eleven: The Historiographical Debate

The scholarship divides on a small number of questions, and knowing who holds what makes the
citations usable.

| Scholar and work | Position |
| --- | --- |
| Bernard Lewis, *Race and Slavery in the Middle East* (1990) | The myth of Islamic racial harmony was manufactured by 19th-century Christians as a reproach to white conduct in the Americas and South Africa. Prejudice regularly defeated pious teaching; the demographic record shows why |
| Ehud Toledano, *Slavery and Abolition in the Ottoman Middle East* (1998) | Ottoman slavery must be understood on its own terms rather than through a plantation template. Slavery was simultaneously subjection and the main ladder of mobility, and the field had barely been studied |
| William Gervase Clarence-Smith, *Islam and the Abolition of Slavery* (2006) | Islamic groups were ambivalent and largely muted on legal abolition, though religious commitment was needed for enforcement. The sharia was less clear on servitude than assumed, and progressive jurists might have reached emancipation internally |
| Paul Lovejoy, *Slavery, Commerce and Production in the Sokoto Caliphate* | Sokoto was among the largest slave societies in recent history; slavery was the backbone of its economy, and Islam substantially shaped the structures sustaining it |
| Chouki El Hamel, *Black Morocco* (2013) | Islamic ideology alone cannot explain social relations in Northwest Africa. Racial slavery operated there, organized around blackness, whatever the law said |
| Kecia Ali, *Sexual Ethics and Islam* (2006), *Marriage and Slavery in Early Islam* (2010) | The jurists theorized marriage through the vocabulary of ownership derived from slavery, and consent consequently never became central to classical Islamic sexual ethics |
| Jonathan A.C. Brown, *Slavery and Islam* (2019) | The "slavery conundrum" is how to reconcile modern moral certainty with scriptural infallibility. God wishes freedom; abolition is the best means to it, and Islam gives no reason against it. Criticized for the treatment of concubinage |
| Haroon Bashir, *Slavery, Abolition, and Islam* (Oxford) | Nineteenth-century Islamic abolitionists used differing arguments and methods to reconcile abolition with the tradition; abolitionist arguments were contested, with defenders invoking the weight of tradition |
| Robert C. Davis, *Christian Slaves, Muslim Masters* (2003) | Between one million and 1.25 million Europeans were enslaved by Barbary corsairs, 1500-1800 — an under-studied traffic running concurrently with the Atlantic trade |

**Where the real disagreement sits.** Not on the facts, most of which are shared, but on three
interpretive questions.

- **Trajectory or equilibrium.** Do the Quranic manumission provisions constitute a program
  aimed at termination that human failure interrupted, or a stable arrangement the tradition had
  no internal reason to abandon? Brown, Bashir's abolitionists, and the *maqasid* reformists
  hold the first; Lewis and the literalists Clarence-Smith describes hold the second, from
  opposite motives
- **Law or practice.** Whether to characterize the institution by its legal rules — the closed
  channels, the manumission instruments, the protected categories — or by what happened, which
  routinely violated them. El Hamel, Lovejoy, and Lewis emphasize practice; confessional
  scholarship and Yaqeen-style institutional writing emphasize rules. Both are describing
  something real, and the argument is largely about which is the appropriate object of
  description
- **Comparison as illumination or as deflection.** Comparative scale figures and the Barbary
  material can be used to situate Islamic slavery in a world where slavery was universal, or to
  deflect criticism by pointing elsewhere. The first is scholarship. The second is not, and it
  appears frequently on all sides — including in accounts that cite the Atlantic trade to
  excuse the Saharan one and accounts that cite the Saharan one to excuse the Atlantic

**The usable rule.** Match the claim to the evidence. Legal texts show what jurists held.
Chronicles and archives show what states and households did. Trade estimates show volume within
wide bands. Manumission instruments show that exit existed; they do not show how often it was
taken. And no figure on either side of the comparison in Part Ten answers the question the
tradition is actually left with, which is the one in Part Nine, section 4.
