# Umar ibn al-Khattab (c. 584–644 CE)

Umar ibn al-Khattab was the second *Rashidun* ("rightly guided") caliph, ruling from 634 CE until his assassination in 644 CE. One of the most influential and powerful figures in early Islamic history, he transformed the regional Arabian state into a transcontinental empire and laid the administrative foundations of Islamic governance.

---

## 1. Title, Family & Early Life

* **Name & Lineage:** Full name Umar ibn al-Khattab ibn Nufayl ibn Abdul-Uzza; *kunya* Abu Hafs. His father, Al-Khattab ibn Nufayl, was from the *Adi* clan of the Quraysh tribe, and his mother, Hantamah bint Hashim, came from the *Banu Hashim* clan.
* **Birth:** Born c. 583–584 CE in Mecca, about thirteen years after the *Year of the Elephant*.
* **Epithet *Al-Farooq*:** The Prophet Muhammad gave Umar the title *Al-Farooq* ("the one who distinguishes between right and wrong"), traditionally because he openly declared his Islam in Mecca, thereby separating belief from disbelief.
* **Pre-Islamic Life (*Jahiliyah*):** One of the few literate men of the Quraysh, Umar was a skilled merchant, an accomplished wrestler and horseman, a composer of poetry, and a tribal mediator with a reputation for rough fairness. He spent roughly half his life in the pre-Islamic era and remembered a harsh upbringing in which his father made him tend camels.
* **Physical appearance:** Tall, muscular, and strong, with a reddish-white complexion; he walked quickly, spoke clearly, and was described as a formidable presence.

---

## 2. Conversion to Islam (c. 616 CE)

* Initially one of Islam's fiercest and most aggressive opponents in Mecca, Umar is said to have set out to kill the Prophet Muhammad with his sword.
* En route he was intercepted by a companion who suggested he first address the conversion of his own family. Arriving at the home of his sister, Fatima bint al-Khattab, and brother-in-law, Sa'id ibn Zayd, he heard Quranic verses being recited and was moved to embrace Islam in 616 CE.
* His conversion was a turning point for the early Muslim community: his strength and social standing gave Islam credibility, and he became the first Muslim to pray openly at the Kaaba, an act other Muslims then followed.
* The Prophet is reported to have prayed, "O Allah! Strengthen Islam with either Abu Jahl or Umar ibn al-Khattab," and tradition holds that Allah chose Umar.
* Umar participated in nearly all of the Prophet's battles and expeditions, including Badr, Uhud, and Khandaq, and served as one of his closest advisers.
* His counsel was repeatedly affirmed by revelation, including his suggestion to make the *Maqam Ibrahim* a place of prayer and his urging that the Prophet's wives be veiled (the formal introduction of the *hijab*).
* **Family ties:** His daughter, Hafsah, married the Prophet Muhammad and later became guardian of the compiled Quran manuscript; his son, Abdullah ibn Umar, became a renowned scholar and narrator of hadith.

## 3. Territorial Expansion (634–644 CE)

Under Umar's ten-year reign, the Rashidun Caliphate expanded at an unprecedented rate—conquering an area of roughly 2.25 million square miles—from present-day Libya in the west to the Indus River in the east and the Oxus River in the north.

### Byzantine Front (Syria, Palestine, Egypt)
* **Battle of Yarmouk (August 636 CE):** A decisive victory under commanders including Khalid ibn al-Walid and Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah, effectively ending Byzantine control over the Levant and paving the way for the capture of Damascus, Jerusalem, and Antioch.
* **Jerusalem (637 CE):** Umar personally traveled to the city to accept its surrender from Patriarch Sophronius, who had refused to yield except to the caliph himself. Umar arrived dressed in simple robes, traveling alone with one donkey and one servant.
* **Conquest of Egypt (639–642 CE):** Initiated by Amr ibn al-As with an initial force of ~4,000 troops, culminating in the fall of Babylon (641 CE) and Alexandria (642 CE), with the new garrison capital at Fustat.

### Persian Front (Sasanian Empire)
* **Battle of al-Qadisiyyah (November 636 CE):** Led by Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas; a decisive victory that opened the way to the Sasanian capital, Ctesiphon.
* **Battle of Nahavand (642 CE):** Known as the "conquest of conquests"; together with the Battle of the Oxus, it completed the collapse of the Sasanian Empire, after which the last shah, Yazdegerd III, fled to Central Asia.
* Historians estimate that over 4,000 cities were brought under Islamic rule during his reign; Umar directed these far-flung campaigns from Medina, some 1,000 km from the front lines, and is regarded as a master strategist.

### Frontier Discipline
* At the empire's edges Umar restrained further advance: he declared the Indus River the eastern frontier of his domain (declining the conquest of Sindh) and rejected proposals to cross the Oxus into Central Asia, prioritizing consolidation over overreach.

## 4. Jerusalem & the Covenant of Umar (637 CE)

* On accepting the city, Umar signed the **Covenant of Umar** (*al-Uhdah al-Umariyyah*) with Patriarch Sophronius, guaranteeing the lives, property, churches, and religious practice of the Christian population in exchange for the payment of *jizya* and the expulsion of Byzantine forces.
* The treaty—one of the earliest formal guarantees of religious freedom—became the standard for Muslim–Christian relations across the former Byzantine Empire; forced conversion was never sanctioned under its terms.
* Umar famously declined Sophronius's invitation to pray inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, reasoning that later Muslims would convert it into a mosque; he prayed instead on the steps outside, where the **Mosque of Umar** was later built.
* According to Jewish tradition, Umar lifted the Byzantine ban that had excluded Jews from Jerusalem, allowing them to reside in and worship at the city—an end to nearly five centuries of Roman/Byzantine exclusion.
* He had the Temple Mount cleared of refuse (which had been used as a dumping ground) and ordered a wooden mosque—a forerunner of the Masjid al-Aqsa—built on the site.

## 5. Governance & Administrative Innovations

Umar is celebrated as a master administrator who institutionalized the early Islamic state. He was the first ruler to style himself *Amir al-Mu'minin* ("Commander of the Faithful").

| Area | Major Innovation / Reform |
| :--- | :--- |
| State Treasury | Established the **Bayt al-Mal** (public treasury, c. 641 CE) as a centralized financial institution managing *zakat*, *jizya*, *kharaj* (land tax), and war booty, and funding salaries, pensions, and welfare. |
| Public Registry | Created the **Diwan**—a register of soldiers and stipend recipients—with payments allocated by precedence in Islam rather than by tribal status. |
| Military | Established the **Diwan al-Jund** (military registry, 637 CE), a professional standing army with regular salaries paid from the treasury. |
| Islamic Calendar | Instituted the **Hijri calendar**, dating from the Prophet's migration (*Hijrah*) to Medina in 622 CE. |
| Administration | Divided the empire into provinces ruled by appointed **Walis** (governors) chosen on merit, not lineage, and held to strict public accountability. |
| Judiciary | Appointed independent **Qadis** (judges), separating the judiciary from the executive so that justice could not be influenced by political power. |
| Infrastructure | Founded military garrison towns that grew into major cities—Kufa and Basra in Iraq, Fustat in Egypt—and built roads, canals, and traveler facilities. |
| Social Welfare | Instituted stipends for widows, orphans, the elderly, and children; oversaw famine relief during the Year of the Drought (638 CE), shipping grain from Egypt and eating only bread and olive oil until his people were fed. |
| Security | Established a policing system for the provinces. |

* His economic and welfare reforms were so effective that historians recorded poverty being largely eliminated during his reign—an era some describe as history's first "welfare state."
* He refused to appoint relatives to positions of authority, and corruption was exceptionally rare under his administration.

## 6. Personal Character & Legacy

* **Justice:** Umar's commitment to justice (*Adl*) became proverbial in Islamic tradition; he would walk the streets of Medina at night, incognito, to check on the welfare of his subjects.
* **Simplicity:** Despite ruling a vast empire, he lived frugally—owning as few as two garments, patching his own clothes, and remaining indistinguishable from his servants.
* **Accountability:** He held himself to the same standards as ordinary citizens; in one famous story he made his own wife pay for lamp oil taken from the treasury for personal use.
* **Legacy:** In Sunni tradition Umar is revered as the second-greatest companion after Abu Bakr and a paragon of justice, piety, and strong leadership; a hadith records the Prophet's instruction to "follow the two after me: Abu Bakr and Umar." His legal opinions (*fatwas*) continued to be cited by scholars for centuries.
* His reign is widely regarded as the golden age of Rashidun governance, and his administrative model influenced Islamic and later global systems of public administration.
* In Twelver Shia tradition, however, Umar's caliphate is viewed negatively, and his role in early Islamic succession remains a central subject of historical analysis across traditions.

## 7. Death & Succession (644 CE)

* **Assassination:** On November 6, 644 CE (26 Dhu al-Hijjah, 23 AH), Umar was stabbed with a double-bladed dagger by **Abu Lu'lu'a Firuz** (also known as Piruz Nahavandi), a highly skilled Persian joiner and blacksmith, while he was leading the dawn (*Fajr*) prayer in the Prophet's Mosque in Medina.
* **Motive:** A Persian slave of the governor al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba, Abu Lu'lu'a was forced to pay a daily *kharaj* tax; when he appealed to Umar to lift it and Umar refused, he sought revenge. Some accounts hold that he died immediately and others that he survived for three days.
* **Aftermath:** In the ensuing chaos, Umar's son Ubayd Allah killed Abu Lu'lu'a's daughter, the former Sasanian officer Hormuzan, and a Christian man from al-Hira before being restrained and imprisoned; the next caliph, Uthman, later acquitted him.
* **Succession:** On his deathbed Umar appointed a consultative council (*Shura*) of six senior companions—Uthman ibn Affan, Ali ibn Abi Talib, Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, Zubayr ibn al-Awwam, and Talha ibn Ubaydullah—to choose his successor, appointing Suhayb al-Rumi as caretaker during the transition.
* The council elected **Uthman ibn Affan** as the third caliph. Umar was buried beside the Prophet Muhammad and Abu Bakr in Medina, at his request to Aisha.
