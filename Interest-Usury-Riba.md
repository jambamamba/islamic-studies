# Interest and Usury: Riba in Islamic Law and History

> **About this document.** A chronological account of *riba* — the Quranic prohibition
> conventionally rendered as usury or interest — from the ancient Near East to the present.
> Part One establishes the pre-Islamic and comparative background, including the Jewish and
> Christian bans, because Islam did not originate the prohibition and the comparison drives
> Part Nine. Part Two covers the Quranic material and the hadith, in the order the verses were
> revealed. Part Three is the juristic settlement, where a short scriptural prohibition became
> a technical body of law and where the schools' disagreement over the prohibition's rationale
> opened the space every later argument occupies. Part Four covers a millennium of practice and
> workaround. Part Five follows the parallel collapse of the Christian ban. Parts Six through
> Eight trace the modern debate, the institutionalization of Islamic banking, and the present
> state of play. Part Nine sets the three traditions side by side and maps the positions.
> Researched Aug. 22, 2026, from academic and confessional secondary literature; primary
> Arabic texts were not consulted directly, and claims are attributed to the scholars and
> studies that make them.

# Part One: Before Islam

## 1. The Ancient Near East and the Classical World

Lending at interest is older than any surviving scripture, and so is the attempt to restrain
it. The pattern in every ancient system is the same, a cap rather than a ban.

The **Code of Hammurabi**, from around the 18th century BCE, set maximum rates by commodity,
capping silver loans at 20 percent and grain loans at 33.3 percent. That is not a prohibition.
It is a recognition that credit was economically necessary and that its price required
regulation.

**Greek** thought supplied the moral objection that Christian and Muslim jurists would later
inherit. Plato and Aristotle both treated interest as unjust, and Aristotle's formulation
proved the durable one, that money is barren and generating money from money is therefore
unnatural. Greek city-states nonetheless regulated lending pragmatically rather than banning
it.

**Rome** followed the same path. The Twelve Tables capped interest at 8 1/3 percent, and later
Roman law permitted 1 percent monthly, or 12 percent a year.

## 2. Judaism

The Torah prohibits interest in three places, at Exodus 22:25, Leviticus 25:36-37, and
Deuteronomy 23:19-20. The prohibition is unambiguous, and it is bounded. It forbids charging
interest to a fellow Israelite while permitting it in dealings with foreigners, and that
insider-outsider structure is the feature that most distinguishes the Jewish ban from the two
that followed.

Jewish law also produced the most enduring of all the workarounds, the ***heter iska***. The
device restructures what is economically a loan as a joint investment, so that the lender's
return is a share of profit in a venture rather than a charge on a debt. It is valid under
*halakha* precisely because risk is nominally shared. Anyone who reads Part Eight of this
document will recognize the structure immediately.

## 3. Christianity

Christianity inherited the Jewish suspicion of interest and removed the exemption. There was
no category of foreigner to whom lending at interest was permitted, and medieval theologians
were close to unanimous that taking interest at any rate from any person was a grave sin.

The conciliar record runs from the Council of Nicaea in 325 and the Council of Laodicea in 364,
which legislated against usury among clergy, to the Third Lateran Council of 1179, which
denied both the sacraments and Christian burial to those who took interest on loans. **Thomas
Aquinas** supplied the mature theory, arguing that usury is contrary to natural law and, with
Augustine, condemning the usurer for selling time — which belongs to God and not to the lender
— and for producing money out of money.

The Christian ban was therefore stricter in principle than the Jewish one and, for a period,
enforced with sanctions no Muslim polity ever matched. Part Five follows what became of it.

## 4. Pre-Islamic Arabia

The practice the Quran addressed has a name in the tradition, ***riba al-jahiliyya***, and the
sources describe a specific mechanism rather than a general market rate.

A debtor who could not repay at maturity was offered a choice, settle now or have the debt
doubled. A hundred dirhams unpaid became two hundred; unpaid again, four hundred. Some
accounts describe the debt as doubled and redoubled annually until it was discharged or the
debtor was ruined. What the sources describe, in other words, is a penalty-compounding regime
applied at default, and the outcome was frequently the enslavement or dispossession of the
debtor.

This matters more than any other fact in Part One, because the whole modern argument turns on
whether the Quran prohibited *this*, or prohibited the wider genus of which this is one
species. The traditional and the modernist readings in Part Six both begin here and diverge
immediately. The usual source-critical caution applies, that the *jahiliyya* is described in
literature written down from the late 8th century onward, in which it functions partly as a
moral foil for Islam.

# Part Two: The Quranic Prohibition, 610-632

## 1. The Four Stages, in Order

The prohibition arrived in four stages across roughly two decades, escalating from
disapproval to categorical ban. The sequence is itself an argument that both camps deploy,
and the verses are given below in revelation order rather than in the order of the text.

### Stage one, Mecca — Surah Ar-Rum 30:39

> Whatever loans you give, ˹only˺ seeking interest at the expense of people's wealth will not
> increase with Allah. But whatever charity you give, ˹only˺ seeking the pleasure of Allah —
> it is they whose reward will be multiplied.

The earliest reference, and not prohibitive in form. It sets riba against *sadaqa* as
spiritually barren against spiritually fruitful, establishing the moral frame long before any
legal rule.

### Stage two — Surah An-Nisa 4:161

> taking interest despite its prohibition, and consuming people's wealth unjustly. We have
> prepared for the disbelievers among them a painful punishment.

Part of a passage of reproach directed at the Jews. The verse presupposes that taking interest
was already forbidden to them, which links the Quranic prohibition explicitly to the Torah
material in Part One, and it pairs riba with consuming wealth unjustly — the closest the Quran
comes to naming the wrong involved.

### Stage three — Surah Ali Imran 3:130

> O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of
> Allah, so you may prosper.

The first prohibition addressed to Muslims, and the pivotal verse for the entire modern
dispute. The Arabic phrase rendered here as multiplying it many times over is *ad'afan
muda'afa*, doubled and redoubled — the exact mechanism of *riba al-jahiliyya*. Whether that
phrase describes the prohibited thing or merely its most flagrant instance is the question
Part Six turns on.

### Stage four, Medina — Surah Al-Baqarah 2:275-280

The final and most severe statement.

> **(275)** Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to
> madness by Satan's touch. That is because they say, "Trade is no different than interest."
> But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest. Whoever refrains — after having
> received warning from their Lord — may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to
> Allah. As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will
> be there forever.

> **(276)** Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful. And Allah does not like
> any ungrateful evildoer.

> **(278)** O believers! Fear Allah, and give up outstanding interest if you are ˹true˺
> believers.

> **(279)** If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger! But if you
> repent, you may retain your principal — neither inflicting nor suffering harm.

> **(280)** If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease.
> And if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you, if only you knew.

Four features of this passage carry most of the argumentative weight.

- **The objection is quoted and rejected.** Verse 275 puts the pro-interest argument in the
  mouths of its opponents — trade is no different than interest — and answers it by
  distinguishing the two categorically rather than by degree. Any modernist reading has to
  contend with the fact that the Quran anticipates and dismisses the analogy between profit and
  interest
- **The language is exceptionally severe.** The warning of war from God and His Messenger in
  279 has no parallel in the Quran's treatment of commercial dealing
- **The remedy is the principal.** Verse 279 leaves the lender the capital sum and nothing
  more, *la tazlimuna wa la tuzlamun*, neither wronging nor being wronged. The prohibition is
  framed as the removal of a wrong, which is what gives the modernist argument its foothold
- **Verse 280 addresses default directly.** A debtor in difficulty gets postponement, and
  remission is better still. This is the precise inverse of *riba al-jahiliyya*, where default
  triggered doubling

## 2. What the Quran Prohibits, and What It Leaves Open

**Settled on the text alone.** Riba is categorically forbidden to Muslims. It is distinguished
in kind from trade, which is permitted. Outstanding riba must be relinquished, leaving the
creditor the principal. Default calls for forbearance or remission, not for an increase. And
the wrong involved is characterized as consuming wealth unjustly.

**Not settled on the text alone.** The Quran never defines *riba*. It does not state a rate, a
threshold, or a set of covered transactions. It does not distinguish consumption loans from
commercial ones, or simple from compound charges, or inflation compensation from real return.
And it does not say whether *ad'afan muda'afa* at 3:130 restricts the prohibition or merely
describes its worst form.

That definitional gap is what Part Three exists to fill, and the manner of the filling is what
Parts Six through Eight are still arguing about.

## 3. The Hadith and the Six Commodities

The hadith corpus supplied the definition the Quran omitted, and it did so by way of a rule
about barter rather than about lending.

The central report names six items.

> Gold for gold, silver for silver, wheat for wheat, barley for barley, dates for dates, and
> salt for salt — like for like, equal for equal, hand to hand. If these classes differ, sell
> as you wish, provided it is hand to hand.

Related reports curse the taker of riba, the giver, the witness, and the scribe, placing all
four under the same condemnation. The Farewell Sermon includes a public renunciation of riba.

The structural consequence of the six-commodities report is the one that shaped everything
after it. The prohibition was no longer only about lending at a stated increase. It now
covered any exchange of two units of the same *ribawi* commodity in unequal quantity, and any
such exchange with delayed delivery. That extension took the prohibition out of the domain of
moral exhortation about exploitation and into the domain of technical contract law, where it
has remained.

# Part Three: The Juristic Settlement, 8th-10th Centuries

## 1. The Two Ribas

The jurists split the prohibition into two categories that answer to different concerns.

| Category | Arabic | What it prohibits | Rooted in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Riba al-nasi'a* | ربا النسيئة | An increase stipulated for deferral — the classical loan at interest, and also deferred delivery in an exchange of *ribawi* goods | The Quran, and the *jahiliyya* practice |
| *Riba al-fadl* | ربا الفضل | An excess in a hand-to-hand exchange of two quantities of the same *ribawi* commodity, however small, and whatever the reason | The six-commodities hadith |

*Riba al-nasi'a* is what a modern reader recognizes as usury or interest. *Riba al-fadl* is
something no Western legal tradition ever prohibited. Trading one weight of gold for a
slightly larger weight, even where the difference reflects a genuine quality difference such
as a wrought ring against plain bullion, falls under it.

The presence of *riba al-fadl* in the law is analytically decisive, and it is routinely
skipped in popular treatments. It shows that the classical prohibition cannot be reduced to a
concern about exploiting desperate borrowers, because no borrower is involved in a spot barter
of equal-quality gold. Something else is being protected — the integrity of the measure of
value, and the exclusion of gain that does not arise from real economic activity.

## 2. The *Illa* Problem

Since only six commodities are named, the jurists had to identify the *'illa* — the operative
legal cause — in order to know what else was covered. They did not agree, and the
disagreement is the hinge of the whole subject.

| School | The *illa* identified | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hanafi | Measurability by weight or volume, combined with unity of genus | The widest extension; any fungible commodity sold by weight or measure becomes *ribawi* |
| Maliki | For the four foods, being a storable foodstuff — both conditions required; for gold and silver, their currency function | Narrower on foods; anchored in preservability |
| Shafi'i | Being a currency (for gold and silver) or being edible (for the rest) | Edibility alone suffices, so a broad class of foods is covered |
| Hanbali | Two transmitted positions, tracking the Hanafi and the Shafi'i views respectively | Internal variation; Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim emphasized the *thaman* or currency function as the efficient cause |

The rationales the jurists offered for these causes cluster around preventing hoarding-induced
scarcity, blocking speculative inequality in exchanges of like for like, and protecting the
integrity of money.

## 3. Why the *Illa* Dispute Matters

Three consequences follow, and each surfaces later in this document.

- **The scope of the prohibition was never fixed by revelation.** It was fixed by juristic
  reasoning from an undefined term and a list of six items, and the schools reached different
  answers. That is an ordinary feature of Islamic law, but it undercuts any claim that the
  boundaries of riba are self-evident from scripture
- **Whether paper money is *ribawi* depends on the *illa* chosen.** The Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn
  al-Qayyim emphasis on currency function is the classical position that most readily extends
  to fiat money, and it is the one on which the modern prohibition of bank interest most
  comfortably rests. A strict reading tied to gold and silver as metals does not extend so
  easily
- **The law became formal rather than purposive.** Once the test is whether a transaction's
  *form* matches a defined category, a transaction restructured to fall outside the category
  passes — regardless of its economic effect. That is the mechanism that produced Part Four
  and, a millennium later, the critique in Part Eight
