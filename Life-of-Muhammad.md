> **About this document.** A chronological account of the life of the Prophet Muhammad,
> assembled from an AI-assisted conversation and reorganized on Aug. 26, 2026. The original
> was a transcript: questions arrived out of order, answers repeated one another, and the
> thread ran on past the Prophet's death into the caliphates that followed. Everything here
> is now sorted by date. Each part opens with a flowchart of its period, and the narrative
> sections follow the flowchart in the same order. Material that belongs to the era after
> the Prophet's death was moved out and is listed under
> [What Moved Out of This Document](#what-moved-out-of-this-document). Sources are cited
> inline at the end of each section and described in [Sources](#sources). The account given
> here is the traditional Muslim biography, drawn from hadith and *seerah* literature written
> down between roughly 120 and 300 years after the events. Where a date or an episode is
> disputed, that is stated rather than glossed. None of it substitutes for the primary texts.

# A Note on Dates

Two calendars run through this document.

**C.E. dates** are the common era. They are the ones most readers can place, so they lead
throughout.

**A.H. dates** are *anno hegirae*, counted from the Hijrah, the migration to Medina in 622 CE.
The Islamic calendar is purely lunar: twelve lunar months come to about 354 days, roughly
eleven days short of the solar year, so A.H. years drift steadily against C.E. years instead of
tracking them. A lunar year can straddle two solar years, which is why some events below carry
a one-year spread.

Dates before the Hijrah were reconstructed after the fact and carry real uncertainty. A "c."
marks the ones that are traditional rather than fixed. The Prophet's birth year, his age at
marriage, and the year of the Night Journey are all reported with variation in the sources. The
Medinan dates are firmer, because the Hijri calendar was in use from that point forward and the
campaigns were recorded close to the time.

## 1. Birth and Lineage

Muhammad was born in Mecca, in the Hejaz region of Arabia, around 570 CE. Islamic tradition
dates the birth to the 12th of Rabi' al-Awwal and names the year *Am al-Fil*, the Year of the
Elephant, after a failed Abyssinian expedition against the city.

He was born into the Banu Hashim clan of the Quraysh, the tribe that governed Mecca and
administered the Kaaba. His father, **Abdullah ibn Abd al-Muttalib**, had died a few months
earlier on a trading caravan to Syria, so Muhammad was fatherless from birth. His mother,
**Amina bint Wahb**, gave birth to him in Mecca and named him Muhammad.

## 2. The Wet Nurses and the Desert

Meccan families of standing sent their infants to be nursed among the Bedouin, on the reasoning
that desert air was healthier than town air and desert Arabic purer than town Arabic. Amina
followed the custom.

* **Thuwaybah** nursed him first and briefly. She was a freedwoman of his uncle Abu Lahab.
* **Halimah bint Abi Dhuayb**, of the Banu Sa'd, took him next. Tradition holds that Halimah and
  her husband prospered while he was in their care. He stayed with her in the desert until he
  was four or five, learning the Bedouin dialect.

## 3. Orphanhood and Guardianship

* **Age six.** Amina took him to Yathrib, later Medina, to visit his father's grave. She fell
  ill on the return journey and died at **al-Abwa**, leaving him a full orphan.
* **Age eight.** His paternal grandfather **Abd al-Muttalib**, who had taken him in and treated
  him with marked affection, died two years later.
* **After that.** His paternal uncle **Abu Talib** took the boy into his own household and
  raised him alongside his children. Abu Talib was not wealthy, but he became Muhammad's
  protector for the next four decades.

## 4. Shepherd, Then Merchant

* **Shepherding.** As a boy he tended sheep and goats for the people of Mecca for a wage. He
  later remarked that every prophet God sent had been a shepherd, a job that teaches patience,
  vigilance, and the handling of a flock that cannot be reasoned with.
* **The first caravan, c. 582 CE.** At about twelve he traveled with Abu Talib on a merchant
  caravan to Syria. Tradition reports that a Christian monk named **Bahira** saw signs of
  prophethood in the boy and warned Abu Talib to guard him.
* **Al-Amin.** Grown, he traded on his own account and built a reputation across Mecca for
  honesty and fair dealing. Meccans called him **Al-Amin**, the trustworthy, and **As-Sadiq**,
  the truthful, and left valuables with him for safekeeping.

## 5. Marriage to Khadijah, c. 595 CE

**Khadijah bint Khuwaylid** was a wealthy Meccan merchant of good family who financed caravans
rather than traveling with them. His reputation reached her, and she hired him to take a major
consignment to Syria.

He ran the journey efficiently and came back with a better return than expected. Her servant
**Maysarah**, who had traveled with him, reported well of his conduct. Khadijah sent a friend
to propose marriage on her behalf.

He accepted after consulting Abu Talib. He was twenty-five. Khadijah is widely reported to have
been forty, though the sources vary on this and some give a considerably younger figure. The
marriage lasted twenty-five years, until her death, and he took no other wife while she lived.

## 6. The Rebuilding of the Kaaba, c. 605 CE

He was thirty-five, five years before the first revelation, when he settled a dispute that had
brought Mecca to the edge of war.

**The damage.** A flash flood tore into the walls of the Kaaba, already weakened by an earlier
fire. The Quraysh decided to take the sanctuary down and rebuild it.

**The deadlock.** Work went smoothly until the **Hajr al-Aswad**, the Black Stone, had to be
returned to its corner. Every chieftain wanted the honor of setting it. The argument ran four
days. Tribes swore oaths of blood, and a civil war inside the sanctuary became a real prospect.

**The arbitration.** An elder, **Abu Umayyah ibn al-Mughirah**, proposed that the next man to
walk through the gate of the sanctuary, Bab al-Salam, would decide the matter. The next man
through was Muhammad. The crowd called out that Al-Amin had come and that they would accept his
judgment.

**The solution.** He spread his cloak on the ground, set the Black Stone in the center of it,
and told each feuding chief to take hold of an edge. Together they lifted the cloth and carried
the stone to the height of the wall. He then took it in his own hands and set it in place.

Every clan had a share in the honor, nobody lost face, and the war did not happen.

## 1. The Cave of Hira and the First Revelation, 610 CE

Approaching forty, troubled by the idolatry and the treatment of the weak in Meccan society, he
began withdrawing for long stretches to **Ghar Hira**, a small cave on Jabal al-Nour, the
Mountain of Light, about two miles from Mecca. He would stay for days at a time. Khadijah sent
food and water up to him.

In Ramadan of 610 CE, while he was in the cave, the angel **Jibril** appeared to him in human
form and commanded: **"Iqra!"** Read. Recite.

He answered that he could not read.

The angel took hold of him and pressed him until he could bear no more, released him, and
repeated the command. He gave the same answer. It happened a third time. On releasing him, Jibril
recited what became the opening verses of Surah al-Alaq:

> "Recite in the name of your Lord who created. Created man from a clinging substance. Recite,
> and your Lord is the most Generous. Who taught by the pen. Taught man that which he knew not."
> **(Quran 96:1-5)**

He repeated the words after the angel, and they held. Then he ran from the cave, shaking.

## 2. Khadijah and Waraqah ibn Nawfal

He got home and called out, **"Zammiluni! Zammiluni!"** Cover me. Khadijah wrapped him in a
blanket and held him until the shaking stopped. He told her what had happened and said he feared
for his own mind.

Her answer is one of the best-known passages in the biography:

> "Never! By God, God will never disgrace you. You keep good relations with your kith and kin,
> you speak the truth, you help the poor and the destitute, you serve your guests generously,
> and you assist those hit by calamities."

She then took him to her elderly paternal cousin **Waraqah ibn Nawfal**, a Christian who had
left Arab paganism years before and who read the Hebrew scriptures. Muhammad described the cave.
Waraqah answered at once:

> "This is the Namus whom God sent to Moses."

Waraqah told him he was the awaited prophet of his people, and added a warning: "I wish I were
young and could live up to the time when your people will drive you out."

Muhammad asked whether they really would drive him out. Waraqah said yes, that no man had ever
brought what he brought without meeting hostility, and that he would support him strongly if he
lived to see it. He died a few days later.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 1, Hadith 3 (the Book of Revelation), narrated by Aisha;
> Sahih Muslim, Book 1, Hadith 310; Quran, Surah al-Alaq (96:1-5).

## 3. The Public Call at Mount Safa, 613 CE

For roughly the first three years the preaching was private, conducted among family and close
contacts and later at the house of **al-Arqam**, known as Dar al-Arqam. Around 613 CE a
revelation directed him to preach openly, and he climbed **Mount Safa** beside the Kaaba to
deliver his first public address.

**The call.** Following the Arab custom for raising an alarm, he called the clans of the Quraysh
by name: "O Bani Fihr! O Bani Adi! O Bani Ka'b!" Clan leaders and members came at a run, and
those who could not come sent someone. They assumed a raid was coming.

**The test.** With the crowd assembled, he asked:

> "If I were to tell you that there is an enemy army in the valley behind this mountain ready to
> attack you, would you believe me?"

They answered without hesitation:

> "Yes! We have never found you telling anything but the truth. You are Al-Amin."

**The message.** Having established that they trusted his word on a matter of life and death,
he gave them the message:

> "Then I warn you that I am a warner to you of a severe punishment ahead. God has commanded me
> to warn my closest kinsmen. I cannot protect you from anything in this world, nor can I promise
> you any share in the Hereafter, unless you say: 'There is no god but Allah.'"

**The reaction.** The mood turned in a moment. His uncle **Abu Lahab** shouted, "May you perish
this day! Was it for this that you gathered us?" and picked up a stone to throw. The crowd broke
up and went home.

**Why it matters.** The Quraysh had just certified his honesty in front of witnesses and then
rejected his message anyway. They did not think he was lying. They were not willing to give up
the idols, the tribal standing, and the pilgrimage revenue that the message would have cost
them.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 65, Hadith 4960 and Hadith 4770, narrated by Ibn Abbas;
> Sahih Muslim, Book 1, Hadith 418; Quran, Surah al-Masad (111), revealed in answer to Abu Lahab.

## 4. The First Migration to Abyssinia, 615 CE

**Why they left.** Five years after the first revelation, the pressure on the early Muslims in
Mecca had passed what the community could absorb. Converts without clan protection took the
worst of it. **Bilal ibn Rabah** was tortured as an enslaved man; **Sumayyah bint Khayyat** was
killed, and is remembered as the first martyr of Islam.

The Prophet told a group to leave the country:

> "If you were to go to Abyssinia, you would find there a king under whom no one is wronged. It
> is a land of righteousness until God provides a relief for you."

**The crossing.** Fifteen Muslims slipped out of Mecca at night and took ship across the Red
Sea. A second wave followed, bringing the total to about eighty men and women, led by the
Prophet's cousin **Ja'far ibn Abi Talib**.

**The Meccan pursuit.** The Quraysh sent two of their best negotiators, **Amr ibn al-Aas**, who
became a Muslim years later, and **Abdullah ibn Abi Rabi'ah**, to the court of the Christian
king of Abyssinia, the **Negus**, with expensive leather gifts for the king's generals and
bishops. Their case was that a group of foolish young men had abandoned the religion of their
fathers, had not adopted the king's religion either, and should be sent home.

**The hearing.** The bishops pressed the Negus to hand them over. He refused to surrender people
who had asked for his protection without hearing them, and summoned the Muslims to court. He
asked: "What is this religion for which you have broken with your people?"

Ja'far answered:

> "O King! We were an ignorant people. We worshipped idols, ate carrion, committed abominations,
> broke the ties of kinship, treated our neighbors badly, and the strong among us devoured the
> weak. We remained like this until God sent us a Messenger from among ourselves... He commanded
> us to speak the truth, to be faithful to our trusts, to be kind to our relatives, and to be
> good neighbors."

**Surah Maryam.** The Negus asked whether they had any of the revelation with them. Ja'far
recited the opening of **Surah Maryam**, chapter 19, which recounts the births of John the
Baptist and Jesus. When the court translator put it into Ge'ez, the Negus wept into his beard
and his bishops wept with him. He drew a line in the dust with his staff and said:

> "In truth, this message and what Jesus brought have come from the very same source of light!"

**The last attempt.** The next day Amr ibn al-Aas told the king that these people said a
monstrous thing about Jesus, and invited him to ask. The Negus recalled the Muslims. Ja'far gave
the answer straight, without softening it: "We say about him what our Prophet brought to us: that
he is the servant of Allah, His Messenger, His Spirit, and His Word which He cast into Mary, the
virgin."

The Negus picked his staff up off the ground and said, "By God, Jesus does not exceed what you
have just said by the width of this stick." He returned the gifts to the Meccan envoys, dismissed
them, and granted the Muslims sanctuary and freedom of worship.

> **Reference:** Sirat Rasul Allah by Ibn Ishaq; Musnad Ahmad, Hadith 1740; The History of
> al-Tabari, Vol. 6.

## 5. The Year of Sorrow and the Journey to Ta'if, 619 CE

**The two deaths.** By 619 CE the Prophet had lost both of the people who made his position in
Mecca survivable: **Khadijah**, who had backed him emotionally and financially for twenty-five
years, and **Abu Talib**, whose clan protection had kept the Quraysh from acting against him
directly. The year is remembered as **Am al-Huzn**, the Year of Sorrow. With Abu Talib gone, the
persecution escalated sharply.

**Ta'if.** Looking for a base and for allies, he walked the sixty miles southeast to **Ta'if**,
a fertile hill city, accompanied only by his freedman and adopted son **Zayd ibn Harithah**. He
put the message to the three brothers who led the dominant tribe, the **Thaqif**, and asked for
protection.

They mocked him:

* One said, "If God sent you as a prophet, I will tear down the cloth of the Kaaba!"
* The second said, "Could God find no one better than you to send?"
* The third refused to speak to him at all: "If you are a true prophet, you are too dangerous
  for me to speak to. And if you are a liar, you do not deserve my words."

They ordered him out. He asked them to keep the visit quiet, since news of the rejection would
embolden the Quraysh. They refused that too.

**The stoning.** Rather than let him leave, the leaders set the town's mobs, children, and
enslaved people on the two of them. They lined the road out of the city in two rows and drove
them for miles. Zayd tried to shield the Prophet with his own body and took severe head wounds.
Stones struck the Prophet's ankles and feet until his sandals were clotted with blood. They
finally lost the crowd and took shelter in a private orchard belonging to two Meccans.

**The prayer.** Sitting under a grapevine, bleeding, he prayed:

> "O Allah! To You alone I complain of my weakness, my scarcity of resources, and my humiliation
> before the people. O Most Merciful of those who show mercy! You are the Lord of the weak, and
> You are my Lord. To whom do You entrust me? To a distant stranger who shows hostility, or to an
> enemy to whom You have given control over my affairs? As long as You are not angry with me, I
> do not care. But Your protection is wide enough for me..."

The owners of the orchard, watching, felt enough pity to send their Christian slave **Addas**
out with a plate of grapes. When the Prophet said *Bismillah* before eating, Addas remarked that
pagan Arabs did not speak that way. They talked. Addas recognized him as a prophet, wept, and
kissed his hands and feet.

**The Angel of the Mountains.** Leaving the orchard, at a place called **Qarn al-Manazil**, a
cloud shaded him. He looked up and saw Jibril with the Angel of the Mountains, who greeted him
and said:

> "O Muhammad! Order me to do whatever you wish. If you want, I will crush them between
> Al-Akhshabayn."

He refused:

> "No, rather I hope that God will bring forth from their descendants people who will worship
> God alone without associating any partners with Him."

Ta'if accepted Islam some years later and went on to produce a line of Muslim scholars.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 59, Hadith 46 (also cited as Vol. 4, Book 54, Hadith 454),
> in which Aisha asks whether he ever had a day harder than Uhud; Sahih Muslim, Book 32, Hadith
> 112 (Hadith 1795); Sirat Rasul Allah by Ibn Ishaq, which preserves the full text of the prayer
> and the account of Addas.

## 6. Re-entering Mecca Under Mut'im ibn Adi

He could not simply walk back into Mecca. Abu Talib was dead, and the new head of Banu Hashim,
**Abu Lahab**, had withdrawn clan protection. A man without a protector could be killed with no
consequence to the killer.

He waited outside the city and sent word asking a Meccan leader to grant him protection. Two
refused. A third, a pagan chieftain named **Al-Mut'im ibn Adi**, accepted. Mut'im had his sons
arm themselves, surround the Prophet, escort him to the Kaaba for the circumambulation, and see
him home. The Prophet never forgot it, and said years later that if Mut'im had been alive and
asked him to release the prisoners of Badr, he would have released them for his sake.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 60, Hadith 23; Sirat Ibn Hisham.

## 7. The Night Journey and the Ascension, c. 621 CE

Not long after Ta'if and the Year of Sorrow came the journey the sources treat as the answer to
that year. The date is not fixed; tradition places it in Rajab, in the tenth or eleventh year of
prophethood, which is roughly 620 or 621 CE.

```
┌────────────────────────┐   ┌────────────────────────┐   ┌────────────────────────┐
│         MECCA          │   │       JERUSALEM        │   │   THE SEVEN HEAVENS    │
│   Al-Masjid al-Haram   │   │   Al-Masjid al-Aqsa    │   │   Sidrat al-Muntaha    │
│  Woken by Jibril and   │──►│ Leads all the earlier  │──►│  Fifty prayers given,  │
│  mounted on the Buraq  │   │   prophets in prayer   │   │    reduced to five     │
└────────────────────────┘   └────────────────────────┘   └────────────────────────┘
```

**Al-Isra, the Night Journey.** Jibril woke him in Mecca and brought him **al-Buraq**, a
heavenly mount. The Buraq carried him to **Al-Masjid al-Aqsa** in Jerusalem, where he led all
the earlier prophets in prayer.

**Al-Mi'raj, the Ascension.** From Jerusalem he ascended through the seven heavens, meeting
prophets at each level, among them Adam, Jesus, John, Joseph, Idris, Aaron, Moses, and Abraham.
He reached **Sidrat al-Muntaha**, the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary, and there spoke with God.

**The prayers.** The five daily prayers were made obligatory during this ascent. The command
came first as fifty. Moses, knowing what people can sustain, sent him back to ask for a
reduction, repeatedly, until the number was five, carrying the reward of fifty.

**The morning after.** He told the Meccans, and they mocked him for it. **Abu Bakr** was told
that his friend claimed to have gone to Jerusalem and back in a night. He answered that if
Muhammad said it, it was true, and he earned the title **Al-Siddiq**, the one who confirms the
truth.

> **Reference:** Quran, Surah al-Isra (17:1) and Surah an-Najm (53:13-18); Sahih al-Bukhari,
> Book 59, Hadith 18.

## 1. The Plot at Dar al-Nadwa

By 622 CE the Quraysh had decided that exile and boycott had failed. Their leaders met at **Dar
al-Nadwa**, the council hall, and settled on assassination. To keep Banu Hashim from taking
blood vengeance on any single clan, they agreed that one young man from every Meccan clan would
strike at the same moment, so that the guilt, and the liability, would be shared by all of them
and actionable against none.

Jibril warned the Prophet of the plot and told him to migrate to Yathrib.

## 2. Ali in the Prophet's Bed

That night, with the assassins around the house, the Prophet told his young cousin **Ali ibn Abi
Talib** to lie down in his bed and cover himself with the familiar green cloak. This did two
things at once:

1. It kept the watchers convinced that he was still inside and asleep.
2. It left Ali in Mecca to return the valuables that Meccans, including the men trying to kill
   him, had deposited with Al-Amin for safekeeping.

He walked out through the front door. The tradition holds that God took their sight from them;
he scattered a handful of dust toward them as he passed, reciting **Surah Yasin (36:9)**: "And
We have put before them a barrier and behind them a barrier and covered them so they do not see."

> **Reference:** Sirat Ibn Hisham; The History of al-Tabari, Vol. 6.

## 3. The Cave of Thawr

He met **Abu Bakr**, and the two went south, the wrong way for Medina, to break the trail. They
hid in **Ghar Thawr**, the Cave of Thawr.

The Quraysh put a bounty of a hundred camels on his head, and professional trackers followed the
footprints to the mouth of the cave. From inside, Abu Bakr could see the trackers' feet and
whispered that if any of them looked down, they would be seen.

The Prophet's answer is quoted in the Quran:

> "Do not grieve; indeed, Allah is with us." **(Quran 9:40)**

Tradition adds that a spider had woven a web across the entrance and a dove had nested in front
of it, and that the trackers, seeing both undisturbed, concluded nobody had gone in and turned
back. The spider and the dove come from the *seerah* literature rather than from the sound hadith
collections, and some later scholars have questioned the chain.

> **Reference:** Quran, Surah at-Tawbah (9:40); Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 65, Hadith 4663.

## 4. Quba and the First Mosque

After three days in the cave and a hard ride north by unused desert routes, he reached the
outskirts of Yathrib. He stopped first at the village of **Quba**, traditionally on 12 Rabi'
al-Awwal, around late September 622 CE, and stayed several days. There he built the **Quba
Mosque**, the first mosque built in Islam.

## 5. Entering Medina and Building the Prophet's Mosque

He entered the city itself a few days later, traditionally on a Friday. Yathrib was renamed
**Madinat un-Nabi**, the City of the Prophet, shortened to Medina.

Crowds lined the streets, and every chief pressed him to lodge with their family. Rather than
choose, and so slight everyone else, he dropped the reins of his camel **Al-Qaswa** and said she
was under orders. She walked through the city and knelt on a vacant plot belonging to two orphan
boys.

He bought the plot and built his house and **Al-Masjid an-Nabawi**, the Prophet's Mosque, on it.
The building was plain: unbaked mud brick, a roof of palm leaves on palm-trunk pillars, a floor
of beaten earth. He carried bricks himself alongside the others.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 63, Hadith 3932; Kitab al-Tabaqat al-Kabir by Ibn Sa'd.

## 1. The Constitution of Medina, 622 CE

Medina was not a clean slate. Its two main Arab tribes, the **Aws** and the **Khazraj**, had
been fighting each other for generations, and several substantial **Jewish tribes** lived in and
around the city. The Prophet arrived as an outsider with a following of destitute refugees.

The document he drafted, **Sahifat al-Madinah**, is widely treated by historians as one of the
earliest written constitutional frameworks anywhere.

| Clause | What it established |
| :--- | :--- |
| One *ummah* | Bound the Meccan migrants (*Muhajirun*) and the Medinan Muslims (*Ansar*) into a single community, distinct from the surrounding societies |
| Religious freedom | Recognized the Jewish tribes as a community alongside the Muslims, free to practice their own faith and law |
| Collective defense | Obliged every signatory, whatever their religion, to fund and defend the city against outside attack |
| No separate treaties | Barred any faction from sheltering the Quraysh or cutting private deals with them |
| Supreme arbitration | Referred serious internal disputes to the Prophet for final decision |

> **Reference:** Sirat Rasul Allah by Ibn Ishaq, which preserves the text of the document in
> full; Kitab al-Amwal by Abu Ubayd (d. 838 CE) on the articles covering taxes, blood money, and
> civic duties.

## 2. The Change of the Qibla, 624 CE

For the first sixteen or seventeen months in Medina, the Muslims prayed facing north toward
Jerusalem, **Bayt al-Maqdis**. In Rajab of 2 A.H., during a congregational prayer, the revelation
came to turn toward the **Kaaba** in Mecca. The Prophet turned mid-prayer and the congregation
turned with him.

The change marked the community out as something other than a variant of the existing
monotheisms of the city.

> **Reference:** Quran, Surah al-Baqarah (2:144); Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 8, Hadith 40.

## 3. The Three Great Battles, 624-627 CE

Moving from a persecuted minority in Mecca to a governing community in Medina brought the
Quraysh conflict into the open.

| Battle | Date | Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Badr** | 624 CE / 2 A.H. | An ill-equipped Muslim force of 313 defeated a Meccan army of about 1,000. The victory established Medina as a regional power. |
| **Uhud** | 625 CE / 3 A.H. | A defeat. Archers left their assigned hill to collect spoils, the Meccan cavalry came round the flank, the Prophet was wounded, and his uncle **Hamza** was killed. |
| **The Trench** | 627 CE / 5 A.H. | A Meccan-led coalition of 10,000 besieged Medina. On the advice of the Persian companion **Salman al-Farsi**, the Muslims dug a trench across the city's open approaches. The coalition could not cross it, and broke up under bad weather and internal disagreement. |

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 64, the Book of Military Expeditions; Sirat Ibn Hisham.

## 4. The Treaty of Hudaibiya, 628 CE

Six years after the Hijrah, the Prophet dreamed that he and his companions were performing
*umrah* at the Kaaba. In March 628 CE he set out for Mecca with 1,400 unarmed men in pilgrim
dress.

The Quraysh refused them entry and met them at a valley called **Al-Hudaibiya**. Instead of
fighting, both sides negotiated. The terms read badly for the Muslims:

* **The truce.** Ten years of peace between Medina and Mecca.
* **The pilgrimage.** The Muslims had to turn back without performing *umrah*, and could return
  the following year for three days only.
* **The defector clause.** A Meccan who fled to Medina and accepted Islam without their
  guardian's permission had to be returned. A Muslim who defected from Medina to Mecca did not.

**Umar ibn al-Khattab** and others found the terms humiliating and said so. The Prophet signed
anyway. On the road back, **Surah al-Fath**, the Victory, was revealed, calling the treaty a
clear victory.

The reason became visible within two years. Peace meant that Muslims and pagans could travel,
trade, and argue about religion without a state of war between them. More people accepted Islam
in the two years after Hudaibiya than in the previous fifteen, which is why the conquest of Mecca
in 630 CE could be carried out almost without fighting.

> **Reference:** Quran, Surah al-Fath (48:1); Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 54, Hadith 19.

## 5. The Letters to the Rulers of the World, 628 CE

The truce with the Quraysh freed the Prophet to look outward. In late 628 CE, 7 A.H., he sent
companions as envoys carrying letters to the rulers of the surrounding powers, inviting them to
Islam. To meet the diplomatic conventions of the day he had a silver signet ring cut, engraved
*Muhammad Rasool Allah*, and sealed each letter with it.

| Recipient | Envoy | Response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Heraclius**, Byzantine emperor | Dihyah al-Kalbi | Interested, respectful, did not convert |
| **Khosrow II**, Sasanian emperor | Abdullah ibn Hudhafah al-Sahmi | Tore up the letter, ordered the Prophet's arrest |
| **The Negus**, king of Abyssinia | Amr ibn Umayya al-Damri | Accepted Islam |
| **Al-Muqawqis**, viceroy of Egypt | Hatib ibn Abi Balta'ah | Courteous, sent gifts, did not convert |
| **Mundhir ibn Sawa**, governor of Bahrain | - | Accepted Islam, and most of his people with him |
| **Al-Harith ibn Abi Shamir**, ruler of Damascus | - | Threatened war on Medina |

### Heraclius, Byzantine Empire

Heraclius was in Jerusalem celebrating his victory over Persia when the letter reached him. He
wanted to check the character of this Arabian claimant, and had Meccan merchants trading in the
region brought before him. **Abu Sufyan**, at that point still the leading opponent of Islam, was
questioned closely about Muhammad's lineage, his honesty, who was following him, and what he
taught. Abu Sufyan, unwilling to be caught lying in front of his own party, conceded that
Muhammad had never lied, had never broken a treaty, and preached the worship of one God and
plain moral conduct.

Heraclius reportedly said he had known a prophet was due, but had not expected him out of
Arabia. He treated the envoy well and sent gifts back. He did not convert. The account has him
judging that his own generals would remove him if he did.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 1, Hadith 7, which preserves the whole exchange between
> Heraclius and Abu Sufyan.

### Khosrow II, Sasanian Empire

Khosrow was insulted that Muhammad's name came before his own in the greeting. He tore the
letter up and threw the envoy out, then ordered his vassal in Yemen, **Badhan**, to send two men
to Medina to arrest Muhammad and bring him to Persia.

Told that his letter had been destroyed, the Prophet said: "O Allah, tear his kingdom to pieces
just as he tore my letter." Khosrow was killed shortly afterward in a palace coup by his own son
Siroes, and the Sasanian state entered the collapse from which it never recovered. Badhan
accepted Islam.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 64, Hadith 4323; The History of al-Tabari, Vol. 8.

### The Negus of Abyssinia

The Negus had already sheltered the Muslim refugees in 615 CE. On reading the letter he came
down off his throne, sat on the ground out of respect for the message, and declared his
acceptance of Islam. He wrote back confirming it. When he died in 630 CE, the Prophet gathered
the companions in Medina and led a funeral prayer in absentia, **Salat al-Gha'ib**, for him.

> **Reference:** Sahih Muslim, Book 7, Hadith 123; Sirat Ibn Hisham.

### Al-Muqawqis of Egypt

The Byzantine viceroy received the envoy warmly, listened to the explanation, read the letter,
and put it away in an ivory box. He did not convert, but he replied favorably and sent gifts:
fine garments, a white mule named **Duldul** that the Prophet rode for years, and two sisters
from a noble Coptic family. One of them, **Mariya al-Qibtiyya**, entered the Prophet's household
and bore his son **Ibrahim**, who died in infancy.

> **Reference:** Kitab al-Tabaqat al-Kabir by Ibn Sa'd; Zad al-Ma'ad by Ibn al-Qayyim.

## 6. The Conquest of Mecca, 630 CE

```

│  Banu Bakr attack Banu   │   │    The truce is void     │   │  10,000 march on Mecca   │
│  Khuza'ah with Quraysh   │──►│   Abu Sufyan's plea to   │──►│  Thousands of campfires  │
│   weapons and fighters   │   │   renew it is refused    │   │ on the surrounding hills │
└──────────────────────────┘   └──────────────────────────┘   └──────────────────────────┘
```

**How the truce broke.** The ten-year truce lasted under two years. A clan allied to the Quraysh,
the **Banu Bakr**, attacked a clan allied to the Muslims, the **Banu Khuza'ah**, at night and
inside sacred territory, killing several men. The Quraysh had supplied weapons and fighters,
which put them in direct breach. **Abu Sufyan** went to Medina himself to ask for the truce to be
renewed. He was refused.

**The march.** In Ramadan of 8 A.H., January 630 CE, the Prophet moved on Mecca with 10,000 men.
He kept the destination secret to prevent a defense being organized, and had the army light
thousands of separate campfires on the hills around the city so that its size would be
unmistakable in the dark.

**The surrender.** Abu Sufyan, seeing what was camped outside, surrendered to the Prophet and
accepted Islam. The Prophet then issued terms of safety designed to make resistance pointless
and a massacre unnecessary:

* Whoever enters the house of Abu Sufyan is safe.
* Whoever locks their own door is safe.
* Whoever enters the sanctuary of the Kaaba is safe.

The army entered from four directions at once. There was one small skirmish against a faction
that chose to fight, and a few deaths. Otherwise the city changed hands without a battle.

**The idols.** He rode to the Kaaba, circled it on his camel, and knocked down each of the 360
idols around the sanctuary with his staff, reciting: "Truth has come, and falsehood has vanished.
Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish" **(Quran 17:81)**.

**The amnesty.** He then gathered the Meccans who had spent twenty years persecuting, torturing,
boycotting, and making war on him, and asked: "O Quraysh! What do you think I am going to do with
you today?"

They answered: "You are a noble brother and the son of a noble brother. We expect only goodness."

He used the words Joseph used to his brothers: **"No blame is upon you today. Go, for you are
completely free."**

The mass pardon is the pivot of the year. Arabia had watched to see what the winner would do,
and conversions followed across the peninsula.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 64, Hadith 4280; Sahih Muslim, Book 32, Hadith 1780.

# Part Five: The Final Years, 630-632 CE

## 1. The Farewell Pilgrimage and Sermon, 632 CE

In March 632 CE, 10 A.H., the Prophet performed his first and only full Hajj, the
**Hajjat al-Wada**. On the plain of **Arafat** he addressed an audience the sources put at over
a hundred thousand, speaking from the back of his camel.

He opened: "O People! Listen to my words carefully, for I do not know whether I will ever meet
you again on this ground after this year."

| Theme | What he said |
| :--- | :--- |
| Life and property | "Your lives and your property are sacred and inviolable to one another until you meet your Lord, just as this day, this month, and this city are sacred." |
| Race and rank | "All mankind is from Adam and Eve. An Arab has no superiority over a non-Arab, nor does a non-Arab have any superiority over an Arab; also a white has no superiority over a black, nor does a black have any superiority over a white, except by piety and good action." |
| Women | "O People, it is true that you have certain rights with regard to your women, but they also have rights over you... Remember that you have taken them as your wives only under Allah's trust and with His permission." |
| Usury and blood feud | Banned *riba* outright, starting with the interest owed to his own family, and cancelled the standing blood feuds of the pre-Islamic period, starting with his own clan's |
| Finality | No prophet and no new religion would follow him. He left two things behind: the Quran and his own practice |

At the close he looked up and asked the crowd, "O people, have I conveyed the message?" The
valley answered, "Yes, by Allah, you have!" He said, "O Allah, bear witness."

The verse traditionally identified as the last legal revelation came shortly after: "This day I
have perfected for you your religion and completed My favor upon you and have approved for you
Islam as religion" **(Quran 5:3)**.

> **Reference:** Sahih Muslim, Book 15, Hadith 159, the long hadith of Jabir covering the entire
> pilgrimage; Musnad Ahmad, Hadith 22978; Sirat Ibn Hisham.

For the prohibition of *riba* announced in this sermon and what became of it, see
[Interest-Usury-Riba.md](Interest-Usury-Riba.md).

## 2. The Death of the Prophet, 8 June 632 CE

Three months after the sermon, the Prophet died in Medina, in Aisha's room, at about sixty-two.

```

└──────────────────────┘   └──────────────────────┘   └──────────────────────┘   └──────────────────────┘
```

The community did not take it well. **Umar ibn al-Khattab** drew his sword and said he would
kill anyone who claimed the Prophet was dead, insisting he had been taken up temporarily as
Moses had been.

**Abu Bakr** came in, uncovered the Prophet's face, kissed his forehead, and went out to the
crowd:

> "O People! If anyone used to worship Muhammad, let them know that Muhammad has passed away.
> But whoever worships Allah, let them know that Allah is Alive and will never die."

He then recited: "Muhammad is not but a messenger. Other messengers have passed away before him.
So if he was to die or be killed, would you turn back on your heels?" **(Quran 3:144)**.

Umar later said it was as though he had never heard the verse before. He dropped his sword.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 86, Hadith 21; Quran, Surah Ali 'Imran (3:144).

## 3. What Followed

While the Prophet's family prepared the body, the leaders of the Medinan **Ansar** met at
**Saqifah Bani Sa'idah** to choose a leader from among themselves. Abu Bakr, Umar, and Abu
Ubaidah went to the meeting, and by the end of it Abu Bakr had been accepted as the first caliph.
The general pledge was taken the next day at the Prophet's Mosque.

That is where this document stops. The succession, the caliphates of Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and

# Sources

## 1. What Each Kind of Source Is

The events above are not preserved as a single contemporary record. They come from three kinds
of text, written down at different removes from the events, and a citation to one does not carry
the same weight as a citation to another.

| Source | Author, date | What it is |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sahih al-Bukhari** | Al-Bukhari, d. 870 CE | Hadith collection compiled against an admission standard, roughly 240 years after the Prophet's death. The most widely accepted Sunni collection. |
| **Sahih Muslim** | Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj, d. 875 CE | The second of the two Sunni *Sahih* collections, compiled on similar principles. |
| **Musnad Ahmad** | Ahmad ibn Hanbal, d. 855 CE | A large collection arranged by companion rather than by topic, admitting reports of mixed grade. |
| **Sirat Rasul Allah** | Ibn Ishaq, 704-767 CE, in the recension of Ibn Hisham, d. 833 CE | The oldest surviving connected biography. A *seerah* work, not a graded hadith collection: it preserves material the hadith critics did not admit, including most of the narrative color. |
| **Kitab al-Tabaqat al-Kabir** | Ibn Sa'd, 784-845 CE | A biographical dictionary of the Prophet and the early generations, with detailed chapters on his youth and trading years. |
| **The History of al-Tabari** | Al-Tabari, 839-923 CE | A universal history that reports variant accounts side by side, often without adjudicating between them. Al-Tabari's stated method is to transmit what he received, not to certify it. |
| **Zad al-Ma'ad** | Ibn al-Qayyim, d. 1350 CE | A much later synthesis of the biography, valuable for organization rather than as an independent witness. |
| **Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum** | Safiur Rahman Mubarakpuri, 20th century | A modern biography that cross-references the classical texts into a single timeline. Useful as a map, not as a source. |

The rule of thumb worth carrying: **the sound hadith collections carry the sayings and the
core events; the *seerah* and history books carry the connected narrative.** Most of what makes
these stories readable, the dialogue at Ta'if, the spider at the cave, the speech at the Negus's
court, comes from the second group. On the genre distinction and what a citation to each can
actually support, see [History-of-Hadith.md](History-of-Hadith.md), Part Four.

## 3. A Caution on Citation Numbers

Hadith numbering is not standardized. The same report carries different numbers in different
printed editions and on different websites, and the older English translations use a book and
volume scheme that the modern numbering does not match. Where two forms of a citation are in
common use, both are given above. A number that does not resolve is usually an edition mismatch
rather than a missing hadith, and the surest way to find a report is to search its opening
words.

# What This Document Does Not Cover

The conversation this document came from moved through the well-known episodes and left gaps.
The following belong to the Prophet's life and are not treated here:

* **The pledges of al-Aqabah**, 621 and 622 CE, the agreements with the men of Yathrib that made
  the Hijrah possible in the first place
* **His household**, beyond Khadijah and Mariya al-Qibtiyya: his other wives, his daughters, and
  his sons who died in infancy
* **The Jewish tribes of Medina**: Banu Qaynuqa, Banu Nadir, and the killing of the men of Banu
  Qurayza after the Battle of the Trench, which is among the most disputed episodes in the
  biography
* **The later campaigns**: Khaybar in 628 CE, Mu'tah in 629 CE, Hunayn and the siege of Ta'if in
  630 CE, and Tabuk in 631 CE
* **The Year of Delegations**, 630-631 CE, when the tribes of Arabia sent representatives to
  Medina
* **His appearance, habits, and daily conduct**, the subject of the *shama'il* literature
* **The rites of the Farewell Pilgrimage** themselves, as distinct from the sermon
