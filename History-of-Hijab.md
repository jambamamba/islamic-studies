# Part One: What the Quran Says

## 1. The Vocabulary Comes First

Almost every argument in this document turns on three Arabic words, and the modern English
word "hijab" does not map onto any of them cleanly. Establishing the vocabulary is not
pedantry — it is the whole disagreement in miniature.

| Term | Arabic | Quranic sense | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Hijab* | حجاب | A curtain, screen, barrier, or partition — a thing that separates two parties | Seven verses, of which only 33:53 concerns women |
| *Khimar* (pl. *khumur*) | خمار / خمر | A head-cloth already worn by Arabian women; the root *kh-m-r* means to cover or conceal | 24:31 |
| *Jilbab* (pl. *jalabib*) | جلباب / جلابيب | An outer wrap or cloak worn over other clothing | 33:59 |
| *Zinah* | زينة | Adornment, finery, or ornament — what is displayed, as distinct from what is private | 24:31 |
| *Awrah* | عورة | Nakedness, that which must not be exposed; the juristic term of art | 24:31, 33:13 |

Two facts about this table drive much of Part Four.

**The Quran never calls a woman's garment a *hijab*.** The noun occurs at 7:46, 17:45,
19:17, 33:53, 38:32, 41:5, and 42:51. In 7:46 it is the barrier dividing Paradise from the
Fire; in 42:51 it is the veil behind which God speaks to a prophet; in 33:53 it is a screen
in a house. In none of these does it denote clothing worn on a woman's head. The garments
the Quran does name are the *khimar* and the *jilbab*, and classical literature calls the
garment a *khimar*, a *jilbab*, or a *kisa'* rather than a *hijab*. The semantic migration
by which *hijab* came to mean "headscarf" is a later development, and the Moroccan scholar
Asma Lamrabet argues that the substitution was not innocent — that swapping a word for a
garment (*khimar*, compatible with a woman moving through public space) for a word for a
partition (*hijab*, connoting seclusion) served interests that wanted women out of public
life.

**The Quran does not describe a garment in enough detail to settle a dress code.** It
regulates two existing garments and states purposes. It gives no measurements, no ruling on
hair as such, and no penalty. That silence is read by traditionalists as a gap the Prophet's
practice fills, and by reformists as evidence that no fixed uniform was intended.

## 2. The Verses

Translations below are from Mustafa Khattab, *The Clear Quran*, as published on Quran.com.
Bracketed Arabic marks the terms at issue.

### The modesty verses: Surah An-Nur 24:30-31

The command is issued to men first, and in identical terms.

> **(30)** ˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the believing men to lower their gaze and guard their chastity.
> That is purer for them. Surely Allah is All-Aware of what they do.

> **(31)** And tell the believing women to lower their gaze and guard their chastity, and
> not to reveal their adornments [*zinah*] except what normally appears [*illa ma zahara
> minha*]. Let them draw their veils [*khumur*] over their chests [*juyub*], and not reveal
> their ˹hidden˺ adornments except to their husbands, their fathers, their fathers-in-law,
> their sons, their stepsons, their brothers, their brothers' sons or sisters' sons, their
> fellow women, those ˹bondwomen˺ in their possession, male attendants with no desire, or
> children who are still unaware of women's nakedness. Let them not stomp their feet,
> drawing attention to their hidden adornments. Turn to Allah in repentance all together,
> O believers, so that you may be successful.

Three features of 24:31 carry the weight of the entire legal debate.

- The verse assumes the *khimar* is already being worn. It does not command women to put one
  on; it commands them to draw the one they have across the *juyub* — the neckline or bosom.
  Whether the head is thereby covered by presupposition or left unaddressed is the central
  exegetical dispute
- The exception clause, *illa ma zahara minha* ("except what normally appears"), is
  undefined. The two earliest reported readings diverge sharply: Ibn Abbas is reported to
  have limited the exception to the face and the palms, while Ibn Mas'ud is reported to have
  limited it to the outer clothing itself, which would exclude even the face. A third
  reading, ascribed to Ibn Mas'ud, al-Hasan al-Basri, Ibn Sirin, and Ibrahim al-Nakha'i,
  takes it to mean whatever is exposed involuntarily, as by wind, for which no blame
  attaches
- The verse distinguishes *zinah* from *awrah*. Adornment is a social and variable category,
  not an anatomical one, and Khaled Abou El Fadl builds his dissent largely on the claim
  that jurists collapsed the two

### The outer-garment verse: Surah Al-Ahzab 33:59

> O Prophet! Ask your wives, daughters, and believing women to draw their cloaks
> [*jalabib*] over their bodies. In this way it is more likely that they will be recognized
> ˹as virtuous˺ and not be harassed. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

This verse states its own rationale, which is unusual and consequential. The purpose given
is recognition and protection from harassment, not concealment as an end in itself. Every
reformist argument that the ruling is contingent on circumstance starts here, because a
command with a stated purpose invites the question of what happens where the purpose no
longer obtains. Traditionalists answer that a divine command does not lapse when a reader
judges its rationale unmet.

### The screen verse: Surah Al-Ahzab 33:53

This is the only verse in which the word *hijab* concerns women, and its addressee is men.

> O believers! Do not enter the homes of the Prophet without permission ˹and if invited˺
> for a meal, do not ˹come too early and˺ linger until the meal is ready. But if you are
> invited, then enter ˹on time˺. Once you have eaten, then go on your way, and do not stay
> for casual talk. Such behaviour is truly annoying to the Prophet, yet he is too shy to ask
> you to leave. But Allah is never shy of the truth. And when you ˹believers˺ ask his wives
> for something, ask them from behind a barrier [*hijab*]. This is purer for your hearts and
> theirs. And it is not right for you to annoy the Messenger of Allah, nor ever marry his
> wives after him. This would certainly be a major offence in the sight of Allah.

The verse regulates etiquette in one household. It is grammatically directed at the male
believers, it names the Prophet's wives specifically, and the *hijab* it institutes is
architectural — a screen in a room, not a garment on a body. Mernissi treats this verse as
the origin of the whole institution and the site of its central misreading.

### The verses addressed to the Prophet's wives: 33:32-33

> **(32)** O wives of the Prophet! You are not like any other women: if you are mindful ˹of
> Allah˺, then do not be overly effeminate in speech ˹with men˺ or those with sickness in
> their hearts may be tempted, but speak in a moderate tone.

> **(33)** Settle in your homes, and do not display yourselves as women did in the days of
> ˹pre-Islamic˺ ignorance. Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and obey Allah and His Messenger.
> Allah only intends to keep ˹the causes of˺ evil away from you and purify you completely,
> O members of the ˹Prophet's˺ family!

Verse 32 opens with an explicit statement of exceptional status: *you are not like any other
women*. Whether the injunctions that follow — including "settle in your homes" — extend to
all Muslim women or apply only to the Prophet's household is a genuine and old dispute, and
it is the seam along which the seclusion tradition and the modesty tradition separate.

### The exemption verse: Surah An-Nur 24:60

> As for elderly women past the age of marriage, there is no blame on them if they take off
> their ˹outer˺ garments, without revealing their adornments. But it is better for them if
> they avoid this ˹altogether˺. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

An exemption keyed to the likelihood of sexual interest, which reformists read as proof that
the rule tracks a social purpose rather than an absolute standard of bodily concealment.

### The framing verse: Surah Al-A'raf 7:26

> O children of Adam! We have provided for you clothing to cover your nakedness and as an
> adornment. However, the best clothing is righteousness. This is one of Allah's bounties,
> so perhaps you will be mindful.

Cited by all sides. It establishes clothing as both covering and adornment, and subordinates
both to conduct.

## 3. What the Verses Settle, and What They Leave Open

Reading the passages together, a short list of each is possible.

**Settled on the text alone.** The Quran commands modesty of dress and of gaze, and it
commands it of men and women in parallel construction. It regulates two named garments
already in use. It states a protective purpose for the outer wrap. It bars the display of
*zinah* to men outside a defined list of relatives.

**Not settled on the text alone.** Whether hair is included; what "what normally appears"
covers; whether the *khimar* command presupposes head covering or merely redirects an
existing garment; whether 33:33's instruction to remain at home binds anyone beyond the
Prophet's wives; whether a command with a stated rationale remains binding where the
rationale fails; and what, if anything, follows for a woman who does not comply, since the
Quran attaches no penalty.

Every position in Part Four is a way of closing that second list. The traditionalist closes
it with prophetic practice and the unbroken agreement of the schools. The reformist closes it
by holding the text to its stated purposes. The historian closes it by asking what people
actually wore, and when, and who was exempt.

# Part Two: How the Practice Formed

## 1. Veiling Before Islam

Veiling is older than Islam by roughly two millennia, and in its earliest documented form it
was a marker of rank enforced by the state.

**The Middle Assyrian law.** The earliest surviving legislation on veiling is a Middle
Assyrian law collection, its material dated to roughly the 14th to 11th centuries BCE. The
provision usually numbered §40 is a sumptuary law of remarkable specificity. Married women,
widows, and Assyrian women were required to cover their heads in the street, and daughters
of status were to be veiled. Enslaved women and prostitutes were forbidden to veil, on pain
of severe penalty: an enslaved woman found veiled had her ears cut off, and a man who saw a
veiled prostitute and failed to report her was himself caned fifty times, given hard labor
for a month, and had his ears pierced and corded.

The logic is worth stating plainly, because it recurs. The veil was not a modesty
requirement applied to women as such. It was a badge of protected status, and its function
depended on other women being denied it.

**Mediterranean and Near Eastern antiquity.** Veiling and the seclusion of upper-class women
were practiced in Mesopotamia, in the Greek and Roman worlds, in Christian Byzantium, and
among the Sasanians, where head covering also carried Zoroastrian associations of ritual
purity. In the Roman convention that Paul addresses in 1 Corinthians 11, women covered their
heads in public and men did not; a doctoral study of that passage places it squarely in the
sumptuary politics of classical antiquity, where the contested question was which women were
entitled to cover. Jewish practice ran parallel, with a married woman's uncovered hair in
public read as a sign of mourning or of public shaming.

**What this establishes, and what it does not.** It establishes that the Quran addressed a
society in which head covering was an existing, class-inflected practice with deep regional
roots — which is why 24:31 can assume the *khimar* rather than introduce it. It does not by
itself show that Islamic veiling is "merely cultural," because a revelation that regulates an
existing custom is not thereby reduced to that custom. Leila Ahmed's tracing of the veil back
to Sumerian and Assyrian class markers and the Yaqeen Institute's argument that revelation
"affirmed and elevated" a pre-existing practice are drawing opposite conclusions from the same
uncontested history.

## 2. The Medinan Context

The narrative sources place the relevant revelations late in the Prophet's life, in a Medina
under military and political pressure.

**The reports on 33:53.** Anas ibn Malik is the transmitter of the account that the screen
verse came down at the wedding feast for the Prophet's marriage to Zaynab bint Jahsh, when
guests lingered in the household. The mainstream reading treats this as a privacy measure for
the Prophet's home. Mernissi treats the same reports as evidence that the *hijab* was a
concession to social pressure at a specific moment, and reads the Companion Umar ibn
al-Khattab as the pressure's principal voice.

**The reports on 24:31 and 33:59.** Several reports describe immediate compliance. Aisha is
reported to have said that when the verse came down the emigrant women cut up their sheets
and veiled themselves with them, and to have praised the women of the Ansar for it, saying
she had never seen women with more faith in the revelation: their men recited the verse to
them and the next morning they prayed wrapped up. Umm Salama is credited with the image that
after the *jilbab* verse the women of the Ansar came out "as if they had crows over their
heads." Aisha is also reported to have lowered her *jilbab* over her face when riders passed
during *ihram*.

**How to weigh these.** They appear in Bukhari and in Sunan Abi Dawud, so they carry real
weight in Sunni argument, and the Yaqeen Institute paper cited below leans on them heavily.
They are also *akhbar* about how a verse was received, and the "crows" image is the kind of
vivid literary detail that comparative work on hadith transmission treats as susceptible to
growth. What they support securely is that head covering was normal among the women of the
Prophet's community and was understood by them as commanded. What they cannot establish on
their own is a determinate garment specification for all times.

## 3. From Report to Rule

The path from these verses and reports to the rule stated in a fiqh manual runs through four
moves, and each is a place where the reformist and traditionalist accounts diverge.

- **Lexical.** The root of *khimar* means to conceal, and the garment was in fact a head
  cloth. Traditionalists take this as settling that the verse concerns the head; reformists
  answer that the verse's operative command concerns where the existing cloth is drawn, not
  whether it is worn
- **Prophetic practice.** The compliance reports and the practice of the Prophet's household
  supply the specification the Quran omits, on the same principle by which the details of
  the five daily prayers are supplied — an analogy the Yaqeen paper makes explicitly
- **Juristic systematization.** Between the 8th and 10th centuries the schools converted
  this into the technical category of *awrah*, defined anatomically and calibrated by
  audience, marital relation, and — decisively — legal status
- **Consensus.** By the classical period the schools agreed on the core rule, and later
  authorities cite that agreement (*ijma'*) as itself dispositive

Note what changed in step three. The Quran speaks of *zinah*, a social category, and of
purposes. The jurists produced a body map. Abou El Fadl's dissent is aimed at precisely this
substitution, and the strongest historical evidence for it is the subject of the next
section.

## 4. The Free and Enslaved Distinction

This is the single most consequential fact in the historical record for the modern debate,
and it is not disputed by any side.

Classical jurists did not require enslaved women to cover as free women did. The dominant
position held that an enslaved woman's *awrah* was substantially smaller, and Kecia Ali
summarizes that jurists disagreed even over whether an enslaved woman's breasts counted as
*awrah* that had to be covered in public. Anas ibn Malik is reported as saying of the enslaved
women in Umar's household that "they used to serve us bare-headed." Traditional sources
explain the distinction by the practical realities of enslaved labor, which involved constant
movement in and out of households and would have made an identical rule a hardship.

Both sides accept the fact and draw opposite conclusions.

- **The reformist inference,** made most directly by Abou El Fadl, is that the distinction
  proves custom and social function shaped the ruling. If the amount of a woman's body that
  must be covered varied with her legal status, then the rule was never a fixed anatomical
  standard revealed once for all women; it was a status marker, continuous with the Assyrian
  logic of Part Two. And a status marker cannot be transposed unchanged into a society without
  the status
- **The traditionalist reply** is that a concession granted to one class on grounds of
  hardship does not unsettle the baseline rule for everyone else, and that the exemption's
  existence presupposes the obligation it exempts from

## 5. Seclusion, Class, and the City

Two developments after the conquests turned a Medinan practice into an imperial institution.

**Adoption from conquered societies.** As Muslim rule extended over Byzantine and Sasanian
territory, Muslims encountered urban upper classes among whom the veiling and seclusion of
women were established. The practice was taken up as an expression of Quranic ideals of
modesty and piety, and the standard scholarly account treats the mature Islamic veiling
complex as a synthesis of Quranic command with late-antique Near Eastern urban custom.

**The Abbasid household.** The Abbasids (749-1258) institutionalized separate women's
quarters, and their court became the model for later Islamic dynasties. The image of the
woman who leaves her house only to be married or buried filtered downward from the ruling
elite as an ideal of purity. Two qualifications matter, because they are routinely lost in
polemic on both sides. Strict seclusion required substantial wealth — separate quarters,
servants, and the ability to forgo a woman's labor — so outside the court only a small
proportion of well-to-do urban families practiced it. And veiling correlated with
urbanization, spreading as the caliphates became more urban and remaining thinner among rural
and nomadic populations.

The upshot is that for most of Islamic history, full veiling with seclusion was an urban
elite practice, not a universal one, and its diffusion downward through society is a modern
phenomenon rather than a medieval one.

# Part Three: The Legal Rulings

This part states what the schools actually hold, because the debate in Part Four is
frequently conducted against a caricature of it in both directions. Technical terms are
given in transliterated Arabic, since that is how the material is indexed in every reference
work.

## 1. The Sunni Schools

The operative category is *awrah* — that which must be covered. It is not a single value. It
varies by four independent factors: the woman's legal status, who is looking, whether she is
in prayer, and which school is asked.

**In prayer,** all four schools hold that a free woman must cover her whole body except the
face and the hands. Hanafis and some Malikis also excuse the feet.

**Before unrelated men,** the schools converge on the same core with differing peripheries.

| School | *Awrah* of a free woman before non-*mahram* men |
| --- | --- |
| Hanafi | The whole body including hair, excepting the face, the palms to the wrist, and the feet below the ankle |
| Maliki | The whole body excepting the face and the hands; covering the hands is recommended |
| Shafi'i | The whole body excepting the face and the palms, on the position identified as most correct |
| Hanbali | The whole body excepting the face and the palms on the preferred of two narrations; the later dominant Hanbali position extends *awrah* to the face as well |

Two observations. First, the hair is inside *awrah* in every school — which is why the
traditionalist case treats the matter as settled and why the reformist case has to argue
against the schools rather than merely between them. Second, the schools agree far more than
they differ, and the differences sit at the extremities: hands, feet, face. There is no
classical school that holds hair need not be covered before unrelated men.

## 2. Twelver Shia and Others

Twelver jurisprudence reaches substantially the same rule by its own route. The standard
position among the *maraji'* is that covering the body and hair before non-*mahram* men is
obligatory, with the face and hands exempt; the face veil is not required. Sayyid Muhammad
Rizvi's treatment, widely circulated in English, argues the case from 24:31 and 33:59 and
frames *hijab* as a mutual responsibility of men and women, pairing the command in 24:30 with
the one in 24:31.

Zaydi, Ismaili, and Ibadi traditions do not produce a materially different rule on covering,
though their differing weightings of *matn* criticism and communal consensus over chain
analysis mean their arguments for it are structured differently.

The practical result is that the obligation is one of the very few substantive rulings on
which Sunni and Shia jurisprudence converge without notable friction, which is a real
difficulty for any argument that the rule is a sectarian or dynastic artifact.

## 3. The Face Veil

The face veil, *niqab*, is a separate question and is genuinely disputed.

The common summary is that the Hanbali school treats face covering as obligatory while the
Hanafi, Maliki, and Shafi'i schools treat it as recommended (*mustahabb*) but not required
unless *fitna* is feared. That summary is serviceable but flattens real internal variation:
each school carries minority positions in both directions, and the "unless *fitna* is
feared" clause does substantial work, since a jurist who thinks temptation is generally to be
feared arrives at obligation by another road.

Institutionally, the most consequential modern statement is Egypt's Dar al-Ifta, which holds
that the *niqab* is a custom rather than a part of religion — while holding, in the same
breath, that the *hijab* is obligatory. That pairing is the mainstream institutional position
across much of the Sunni world, and it matters for Part Four: the same authority that
declares the hair obligation closed declares the face obligation open, which concedes that
the boundary between religion and custom in dress is a question the tradition itself asks.

## 4. What the Consensus Claim Rests On

Both sides invoke *ijma'*, so it is worth being exact about what the claim is and what
answers it.

**The claim.** The Yaqeen Institute paper on whether hijab is religious or cultural sets out
the standard structure. Consensus is not the premise but the last step: begin with the
Quranic command, clarify it by the Sunnah, the practice of the Companions, and lexical
analysis, note that individually equivocal indicators reinforce each other cumulatively, and
then treat the unbroken agreement of the schools as a stamp of approval that raises a strong
argument to a definitive one. The accompanying methodological move is a burden-shift: a
divine command is presumed binding, so whoever says a command is non-binding culture must
prove it.

**The replies.** Three are made, and they are of unequal force.

- *The consensus is real but not self-validating.* Classical *usul* itself treats most legal
  rulings as resting on solitary reports yielding probability rather than certainty, and
  agreement among jurists who shared a social world is weaker evidence than the number of
  jurists suggests. This is the reformists' strongest structural point and it is not specific
  to hijab
- *The consensus was not on a single rule.* The schools agreed the hair was *awrah* for free
  women and agreed it was not for enslaved women. A consensus that varies by legal status is
  a consensus about social role, not about anatomy
- *Consensus claims are contested in fact.* Contemporary dissenters — Abou El Fadl, Gamal
  al-Banna, Muhammad Sa'id al-Ashmawi, Javed Ahmad Ghamidi, Abdullah al-Judai — hold either
  that the classical evidence does not bear the weight put on it or that the ruling was
  contingent. Traditionalists answer, fairly, that a handful of 20th and 21st-century
  dissents does not retroactively dissolve a millennium of agreement

The disagreement is therefore not really about whether the schools agreed. They did. It is
about how much a classical consensus can settle, which is a question in legal theory, not in
history.

# Part Four: The Debate

Each position is given below in the form its own proponents would recognize, followed by the
strongest objection to it. Nothing here settles the question.

## 1. The Traditionalist Case

**The core argument.** The Quran commands it, the Prophet's community understood and
practiced it, and every school of law affirmed it for a thousand years. That is as strong as
a legal argument in Islam gets.

The evidence, as the Yaqeen Institute paper assembles it:

- **Text.** 24:31 commands the *khumur* be drawn over the *juyub*, and the root of *khimar*
  means to conceal. A garment named for concealment, worn on the head, is the subject of the
  command
- **Reception.** The compliance reports are numerous and early: the women tore their
  waist-wraps and covered themselves; the women of the Ansar came out veiled the next
  morning. A community that responded that way understood the verse as this generation of
  dissenters does not
- **Analogy.** The Quran does not detail the *rak'ahs* or postures of the five daily prayers
  either. Detail supplied by prophetic practice rather than by the text is the normal
  structure of Islamic law, not an anomaly to be held against this ruling
- **Culture is not a defeater.** Islamic law treats inherited custom in one of three ways: it
  prohibits it, it reforms it with restrictions, or it affirms and elevates it. Head covering
  is a case of the third. That the practice existed before Islam says nothing about whether
  revelation made it binding
- **Consensus.** All classical schools, Sunni and Shia, affirmed the obligation. Dar al-Ifta
  in Egypt states the position in contemporary institutional form: obligatory on every adult
  Muslim woman, the whole body but the face and hands, established by Quran, Sunnah, and
  consensus

**The strongest objection.** The argument's weakest joint is the free and enslaved
distinction, because it shows the classical rule was calibrated to social status and
therefore was not the timeless anatomical standard the modern statement of it implies. The
analogy to prayer also cuts both ways: prayer's details are transmitted as *mutawatir*
communal practice, whereas the specification of a woman's dress rests on solitary reports
and juristic reasoning. Traditionalist authorities also concede, through the *niqab* ruling,
that some dress practice is custom rather than religion — which means the religion-versus-
custom line is one the tradition draws by argument, not one it reads off the text.

## 2. Mernissi and *The Veil and the Male Elite*

Fatima Mernissi (1940-2015) was a Moroccan sociologist trained at the Sorbonne and Brandeis.
*Le harem politique: Le Prophète et les femmes* appeared with Albin Michel in 1987 and was
banned in Morocco and several Arab states for over a decade; a fatwa was issued against her
in the early 1990s. Mary Jo Lakeland's English translation appeared in 1991 as *The Veil and
the Male Elite: A Feminist Interpretation of Women's Rights in Islam*. An authorized Arabic
edition appeared in 2022 and was barred from the Riyadh Book Fair in September 2023 and the
Cairo Book Fair in February 2024 — which is to say the book remains live rather than
historical.

**The thesis.** Islam's original impulse was egalitarian, and the subordination of women in
Muslim societies is the work of a male elite that captured the sources afterward. Mernissi
takes 33:35, which addresses believing men and believing women in identical terms and
promises them the same reward, as the statement of that original impulse — an assertion of
individual sovereignty before God for both sexes. The tradition that followed, she argues,
substituted the law of tribal violence for that principle and embedded pre-Islamic tribal
misogyny in the fabric of Islamic tradition.

**The method.** She works with the classical apparatus rather than around it. Hadith science
requires that a transmitter be both qualified and reliable; she takes up the biographical
literature and applies its own criteria to the transmitters of reports used against women.
She argues explicitly that this is not a departure from Muslim practice but an instance of
it, since Muslims are obliged to question, including reports labeled authentic. That framing
is deliberate: she is addressing practicing Muslims who would otherwise dismiss her, not only
academics.

**The two hadith.** She singles out two as the central weapons in the arsenal against
women's equality.

- *Abu Bakra's report*, "Those who entrust their affairs to women will never know
  prosperity." Mernissi's case is chronological and biographical. Abu Bakra produced the
  report during or after the Battle of the Camel in 656, roughly a quarter-century after the
  Prophet's death, at the moment Aisha's forces had been defeated and Ali had retaken Basra —
  and the report is exactly what a man in Abu Bakra's position needed in order to make his
  peace with the victor. She adds that the biographical literature records him as having been
  convicted of false testimony
- *Abu Hurayra's report* placing women in the same category as animals in relation to sacred
  space and the annulment of prayer. Mernissi argues that Abu Hurayra likewise had personal
  reasons and a reputation for unreliability, and she notes that the Prophet did not treat
  women's bodies as ritually polluting

**The account of the *hijab*.** This is the book's core and the reason it belongs at the
center of this document. Mernissi identifies 33:53 as the founding text of the institution
and argues it has been systematically misread. On her reading:

- The verse addresses the male believers, not women. It tells men to speak to the Prophet's
  wives from behind a screen. It is a rule of etiquette for one house, occasioned by guests
  who would not leave a wedding feast
- The Prophet's own practice ran the other way. Mernissi reads his life as including his
  wives in public discourse and public space, and his revelations on inheritance as a break
  with a tribal past in which women were property
- The *hijab* "descended" at a moment of acute crisis. Medina was militarily exposed, the
  Prophet needed the fighting strength of conservative tribal forces, and internal dissenters
  — the Hypocrites — were targeting his wives in the street. The egalitarian program cost too
  much politically to hold
- Umar ibn al-Khattab is the pivot. Mernissi presents him as the conservative pressure to
  which the Prophet finally yielded, veiling his wives for their protection and permitting a
  broader retreat on women's rights as the price of the community's survival
- Therefore the veil is a defeat, not an ideal. The Prophet envisioned a society in which
  violence was illegitimate and individual self-control made the surveillance of women
  unnecessary; the *hijab* substituted supervision for self-control, and Companion resistance
  to that principle of individualism diluted the message

**What the argument requires.** It requires that a specific verse be read narrowly against
its later juristic expansion, that certain canonical reports be treated as politically
motivated fabrications, and that the Prophet's biography be read as a trajectory interrupted
rather than a completed model. Each is a live claim; none is absurd; all are contestable.

## 3. The Criticisms of Mernissi

Taking the book seriously means stating the objections, which are substantial and come from
sympathetic readers as often as hostile ones.

- **The source base is narrow.** One academic review notes that her range of sources pales
  against comparable work in the field, citing Fred Donner's *Narratives of Islamic Origins*
  as the standard, and that the narrowness produces sweeping and unattributed assertions.
  The reviewer singles out her claim that the tribal armies of Islam were "strongly
  homosexual" as an egregious instance
- **The structure is confused.** The same review finds the book repetitive and unclear in
  organization, and reads it as a call to arms for Muslim women at least as much as a work of
  scholarship — not a disqualification, but a caution about how to cite it
- **It flattens Sunni and Shia difference.** By treating the tradition as one male elite, she
  passes over interpretive differences between Sunni and Shia readings of Aisha, which
  D.A. Spellberg's *Politics, Gender and the Islamic Past* shows to be exactly where the
  material on women's authority is most revealing. The effect is to lose nuance that would
  have strengthened the feminist argument
- **The Quran resists the thesis in places.** A second reviewer observes that some Quranic
  content sits awkwardly with a claim of original equality — the unequal inheritance shares,
  and the *hijab* itself — and suggests these read more naturally as compromises than as
  ideals. The verdict offered is the Scottish "not proven": she shows Islam is compatible
  with equality, not that it actively favors it
- **The methodology is contested on its own terms.** Muslim critics argue her handling of the
  sources departs from established *usul*, particularly in reading meanings away from their
  ordinary sense, and that a genealogical critique of transmitters selected for the
  conclusions they support is not the same as applying *jarh wa ta'dil* impartially
- **The historical readings are sometimes anachronistic.** Even reviewers who assess the book
  positively note interpretations that are narrow or that import modern categories into the
  7th century

The fair summary is that Mernissi's reading of 33:53 as an etiquette rule for one household
is textually strong and widely accepted even by scholars who reject her larger thesis, while
her reconstruction of the Prophet's interrupted egalitarian program is a historical argument
that outruns its evidence.

## 4. Leila Ahmed: Colonial Genealogy and Quiet Revolution

Leila Ahmed's two books make an argument that is historical rather than juridical, and it
has probably shaped academic discussion of the veil more than any other single body of work.

**In *Women and Gender in Islam: Historical Roots of a Modern Debate* (Yale, 1992),** Ahmed
traces the discourse on women from pre-Islamic Arabia through the classical period to modern
Egypt, and makes two claims that recur throughout Part Five. The first is that the veil's
*meaning* has changed radically: in Sumerian and Assyrian practice and through the premodern
Islamic centuries it signified status and male protection, an emblem of honor for
upper-class women, and only at the end of the 19th century did it become the master symbol
of Muslim women's oppression. The second is that this transformation was largely a colonial
production. Under Lord Cromer's administration in Egypt, unveiling was made the test of
whether Muslim men could be civilized — and Cromer, on returning to Britain, founded the
Men's League for Opposing Woman Suffrage. Ahmed's term for the resulting formation is
colonial feminism: a concern for native women deployed as an instrument of rule rather than
of emancipation. Her judgment on Qasim Amin, that he was a "son of Cromer and colonialism,"
is the sharpest expression of it.

**In *A Quiet Revolution: The Veil's Resurgence, from the Middle East to America* (Yale,
2011),** she turns to the question the first book left open: why the veil came back. Ahmed is
candid that she had expected it to disappear and was disturbed when it did not, and the book
is in part a correction of her own earlier expectation and of what she calls the near
hysterical reaction of commentators who share her secular politics. She argues that two
tendencies were present in the Prophet's teaching from the start and remain in tension — a
vision of the spiritual and moral equality of all humans, and a hierarchical structure of
gender relations — and that the revival cannot be read simply as the second defeating the
first. Her more provocative finding is that Islamist movements, with their commitments to
activism for the poor and for social justice, have often been the vehicles through which
women entered public activism, including on women's rights.

**The objection.** Ahmed's colonial genealogy is now close to consensus among historians, and
the objection is not to its accuracy but to its use. Demonstrating that the veil's modern
symbolism was manufactured by colonialism establishes nothing about whether the underlying
obligation is binding, and it can function rhetorically to place the question beyond
criticism — as though any objection to veiling inherited Cromer's motives. Traditionalists
also note that a history which grounds the practice in shifting social meaning rather than in
divine command is not, on their terms, an argument for it.

## 5. The Reformist Juristic Case

This is the position that argues from inside Islamic legal reasoning that head covering is
not obligatory. It is distinct from the anthropological defense in section 6 and from
Quranism, and it is where the live scholarly controversy sits.

**Khaled Abou El Fadl** (UCLA) issued fatwas in 2016 and 2017 concluding that hair covering
is not obligatory and that no sin attaches to a Muslim woman in America who does not cover.
His reasoning has four steps. He disputes that the evidence establishes what the *khimar*
historically covered, holding that it does not definitively prove face or hair. He separates
*awrah* from *zinah*, arguing that what counts as *zinah* varies by time and place and that
jurists conflated a social category with an anatomical one. He points to the free and
enslaved distinction as demonstrating that custom shaped the classical rulings, so those
rulings were contextual rather than permanent. And he holds 33:59 to its stated purpose:
where the headscarf increases a woman's visibility and risk rather than protecting her from
harassment, the rationale the verse gives has inverted, and safety and spiritual substance
take priority over formal compliance with a contested dress code.

**Javed Ahmad Ghamidi**, a student of Amin Ahsan Islahi, holds that Islam prescribes no dress
code for women beyond modest and dignified clothing, that the popular head covering is past
culture wrongly reported as religious duty, and that women should be free to choose. He
allows that a scarf or *niqab* may be the better option where a woman's safety requires it,
which locates him close to Abou El Fadl on rationale though he reaches it from a different
hermeneutic.

**Asma Lamrabet** argues the semantic case set out in Part One: that *hijab* in the Quran is
a curtain and concerns the Prophet's household, that *khimar* in 24:31 refines an existing
Arabian garment for modesty, and that the later substitution of the first term for the second
served political interests in excluding women from public life. She adds a theological
argument from 2:256 — that framing the headscarf as compulsory sits badly with "no compulsion
in religion" — and objects to reducing Islamic ethics to female dress.

**Muhammad Sa'id al-Ashmawi**, an Egyptian judge, treated the *hijab* as a socio-political
construct rather than an Islamic obligation, arguing from contextual reading of the verses
together with criticism of the relevant hadith. **Gamal al-Banna** likewise held it cultural
rather than mandated, as part of a broader program subordinating hadith to Quran; he rejected
635 reports in Bukhari and Muslim as contradicting the Quran. Others in this range include
Abdullah al-Judai, Zaki Badawi, Khalid Zaheer, and Shehzad Saleem.

**The objection.** The traditionalist reply is that this case has to explain away the
compliance reports, a millennium of unbroken juristic agreement, and the lexical force of
*khimar*, and that its reliance on rationale-tracking would destabilize a great many settled
rulings if applied consistently. There is also a sociological objection worth stating: the
reformist argument arrives overwhelmingly from scholars writing in or for Western contexts,
which invites the charge that the conclusion preceded the reasoning. Institutionally the
dissent has gained little ground — Dar al-Ifta's position is unchanged, and Abou El Fadl
reports that Ali Gomaa declared a woman who denies the obligation to be an unbeliever, which
indicates how far the mainstream is from treating the question as open.

## 6. The Anthropological Defense

A distinct body of work defends veiling without arguing about *fiqh* at all. Its claim is
that the Western reading of the veil as a self-evident emblem of subjugation fails as
description, and that what women wearing it are actually doing has to be established
empirically rather than assumed.

- **Fadwa El Guindi**, *Veil: Modesty, Privacy and Resistance* (1999), draws on fieldwork in
  late-1970s Egypt where young, educated, often progressive women took up the veil against
  their families' wishes. Her argument is that the practice cannot be reduced to modesty,
  seclusion, honor, and shame; it operates on notions of self, body, community, privacy, and
  space, and functioned in that setting as an affirmation of cultural identity and a
  de-marginalizing move that let women occupy public space rather than withdraw from it
- **Saba Mahmood**, *Politics of Piety* (2005), presses the theoretical point: the liberal
  assumption that agency means resistance to norms cannot see what women in the Egyptian
  mosque movement are doing, which is cultivating a self through discipline. On this account
  the question "is she choosing it or coerced?" is malformed
- **Lila Abu-Lughod**, *Do Muslim Women Need Saving?* (Harvard, 2013), attacks the rescue
  narrative directly, arguing that the veil is a complex symbol shaped by colonial history,
  political narrative, and local moral systems, and that global discourse flattens it into a
  sign of oppression that local ethnography does not support
- **Katherine Bullock**, *Rethinking Muslim Women and the Veil* (IIIT, 2002), makes the
  compatibility-with-modernity argument on the basis of interviews with Muslim women in
  Canada, including the claim that in consumer capitalist cultures covering can be
  experienced as release from a punishing beauty economy
- **Elizabeth Bucar**, *Pious Fashion* (Harvard, 2017), based on fieldwork in Tehran,
  Yogyakarta, and Istanbul, concludes that pious dress depends far more on local aesthetic
  and moral values than on religious doctrine. Her details are the argument: ripped jeans
  are more common than black abayas in Tehran, the headscarf is historically recent in
  Yogyakarta and reads there as cosmopolitan, and Istanbul's modest dress is strongly shaped
  by Western fashion

**The objection.** This literature is strong on what veiling means to those who choose it and
structurally weak on the case of those who do not. Ethnography conducted among women who have
adopted a practice will find meaning in it, and the method has little to say to a woman in
Tehran or Kabul for whom the question is not meaning but law. Marnia Lazreg presses the
harder version: that treating the veil as pure self-expression obscures organized campaigns
promoting it. And there is a category problem — showing that a practice is experienced as
liberating establishes nothing about whether it is obligatory, which is the question the
jurists are arguing about.

## 7. The Feminist Critique of the Veil

The critical position is often caricatured as Western, so it is worth taking its
best-credentialed insider version.

**Marnia Lazreg**, *Questioning the Veil: Open Letters to Muslim Women* (Princeton, 2009),
writes as an Algerian sociologist of Middle East women's studies, combining her upbringing in
a Muslim family with interviews. She addresses the reader directly, letter by letter, and
refuses both demonization and idealization. Her points:

- The veil is not among the five pillars, and its elevation to the test of a woman's faith is
  a modern distortion of priorities
- Piety is not by itself a sufficient justification, since the question is what the practice
  does
- Veiling carries psychological costs for the wearer and for those around her, and the costs
  fall hardest on young girls
- The claim that the veil can rejuvenate Muslim civilization does not survive examination
- The trend is not spontaneous. Lazreg documents an organized and growing campaign of
  literature, DVDs, and video teaching women their supposed rights under the veil, much of it
  designed by men

**The objection.** Critics answer that Lazreg's psychological claims are the weakest part of
the case, resting on interview material rather than systematic evidence, and that
documenting a promotional apparatus does not establish that individual women are dupes of it
— an inference the anthropological literature exists to resist. The deeper objection is the
mirror of the one made against the anthropologists: an argument built from the experience of
women who found the practice oppressive generalizes no better than one built from women who
found it liberating.

## 8. The Spectrum Is Wider Than Two Camps

The debate is usually staged as obligation versus oppression. At least six positions are
distinguishable, and the interesting disagreements are between adjacent ones.

| Position | Claim about the ruling | Representative figures and institutions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maximalist traditionalist | Face and body must be covered; *niqab* is obligatory | Later dominant Hanbali position; contemporary Salafi scholarship; the Taliban's decrees as state policy |
| Mainstream traditionalist | Hair and body obligatory, face and hands exempt; *niqab* is custom | The four schools; Twelver *maraji'*; Egypt's Dar al-Ifta; Yaqeen Institute |
| Contextualist within the tradition | The obligation is real but its scope tracks purpose and custom, and can lapse where the rationale inverts | Khaled Abou El Fadl, Abdullah al-Judai |
| Modesty without a garment | The Quran requires modest and dignified dress; no specific head covering is prescribed | Javed Ahmad Ghamidi, Asma Lamrabet, Gamal al-Banna, Muhammad Sa'id al-Ashmawi |
| Historicist and feminist critique | The practice is a historically produced instrument of male authority, whatever its scriptural pretext | Fatima Mernissi, Marnia Lazreg; Leila Ahmed on the genealogy |
| Ethnographic defense | The obligation question is not the interesting one; what matters is what the practice does for those who take it up | Fadwa El Guindi, Saba Mahmood, Lila Abu-Lughod, Katherine Bullock, Elizabeth Bucar |

Two things follow. The live controversy is between rows two, three, and four — that is,
among people arguing inside Islamic legal reasoning — and not between rows one and five,
which mostly talk past each other. And rows five and six are not on the same axis as the
others at all: one is a historical claim, the other an ethnographic one, and neither answers
the juristic question directly, which is why arguments across those rows so often fail to
connect.

## 9. Where the Disagreement Actually Sits

Most of the factual record is shared. All sides can accept that veiling predates Islam, that
the Quran regulates existing garments and states purposes, that the Prophet's community
covered, that the schools agreed for a millennium, that they exempted enslaved women, that
the modern symbolism was forged under colonialism, and that the practice was voluntarily
revived from the 1970s. The disagreements are of four other kinds.

- **What kind of thing a garment ruling is.** If dress rules are *'ibadat*, acts of worship
  whose point is obedience, then a stated rationale is illustrative and the ruling stands
  whatever the circumstances. If they are *mu'amalat*, socially instrumental rules, the
  rationale governs and the ruling moves with it. Nothing in the text settles which one this
  is, and every reformist and traditionalist argument on rationale is a proxy for this
- **How much a classical consensus settles.** Traditionalists treat *ijma'* as raising a
  strong argument to a definitive one; reformists treat agreement among socially similar
  jurists as weaker than its unanimity suggests, and point out that this particular consensus
  varied by legal status
- **Who decides.** Accepting the mainstream ruling means accepting the authority of the
  scholarly guild that maintains it. Rejecting it relocates the decision to the individual
  woman — which is why the dissent fragments in practice and why institutions treat it as a
  threat rather than an error
- **Symmetrical difficulty.** The traditionalist has to explain why a rule with a stated
  protective purpose binds where it demonstrably increases harm, and has to live with the
  free and enslaved distinction inside the consensus being invoked. The critic has to explain
  the millions of women who adopted the practice without compulsion, and has to avoid the
  position that those women are simply mistaken about their own lives — which is the charge
  the anthropological literature exists to press

# Part Five: The Politics, 1899 to the Present

The modern history of the hijab is not primarily a history of theology. It is a history of
states — colonial and postcolonial, secular and Islamist — using women's clothing to make
claims about civilization, sovereignty, and legitimacy. The pattern that emerges is that
compulsion has run in both directions, and roughly as often.

## 1. 1899: The Debate Begins Under Occupation

Qasim Amin published *Tahrir al-Mar'a* (*The Liberation of Woman*) in Cairo in 1899, followed
by *al-Mar'a al-Jadida* in 1900. Amin argued from Islamic premises that improving women's
status was a precondition for Egypt's liberation from foreign domination, and in doing so
moved the question of women from a marginal concern to a national one. The book was
immediately controversial and has remained so for opposite reasons in different generations.

The critique that has stuck is Leila Ahmed's. Amin wrote under a British occupation whose
consul-general, Lord Cromer, made the veil the emblem of Egyptian backwardness — and who on
returning to Britain founded the Men's League for Opposing Woman Suffrage. Ahmed's charge
that Amin was a "son of Cromer and colonialism" is that his argument adopted the colonizer's
framing wholesale, and a substantial body of scholarship reads *Tahrir al-Mar'a* as
substituting Western-style male dominance for Islamic-style male dominance rather than
displacing dominance. Against this, other scholars have argued the reception history is more
complicated than the "colonial feminism" verdict allows.

**1923.** Returning from the International Woman Suffrage Alliance congress in Rome, Huda
Sha'arawi and Saiza Nabarawi removed their face veils at the Cairo railway station in a
public act that drew wide press attention. Sha'arawi founded the Egyptian Feminist Union the
same year, campaigning for suffrage, personal status reform, and women's education. The act
was indigenous, not colonial — which is the standing complication for any account that treats
unveiling as purely an imposition from outside.

## 2. Forced Unveiling

Three states banned the veil, and the manner of the banning shaped the reaction for
generations.

- **Iran, 1936.** Reza Shah issued the *kashf-e hijab* decree on Jan. 8, 1936, banning all
  Islamic veils including the chador, and enforced it aggressively. He regarded traditional
  dress as a mark of backwardness and also compelled men into Western clothing and hats.
  Enforcement lasted from 1936 to 1941, after which women dressed as they chose for four
  decades. Scholars attribute the decree variously to the Shah's personal convictions,
  Ataturk's influence, and calculations about clerical power
- **Turkey.** The Turkish transition was gradual and largely optional rather than a single
  prohibition, which is the standard contrast drawn with Iran; the Hat Law of 1925 targeted
  men's headgear, and restrictions on women's headscarves developed later through
  institutional rules governing schools, universities, and public employment
- **Tunisia, 1957.** Habib Bourguiba removed the *safsari* from the heads of women before a
  large crowd on Aug. 3, 1957, framing it as liberation on the eve of independence. Islamic
  dress was restricted under Bourguiba and Ben Ali and permitted again after 2011; in 2019
  Tunisia banned the face veil in government offices as a security measure

The Iranian case is the one that mattered most, because the bitterness it created was
available for mobilization in 1979.

## 3. The Veil as Anticolonial Weapon

The French campaign in Algeria produced the clearest demonstration that the veil's political
meaning is set by whoever attacks it. French authorities staged public unveiling ceremonies as
emblems of a benevolent emancipation, indicting Algerian society as medieval in the same
gesture.

Frantz Fanon's chapter "Algeria Unveiled," in *A Dying Colonialism* (1959), analyzes the
result. Because the colonizer fixed on the veil's removal, the liberation front made the veil
a weapon — and then, tactically, made its absence one. Women carrying messages, money, and
arms removed the *haik* precisely because an unveiled woman read as pro-French and therefore
harmless, moving freely through European districts of the cities. Fanon's general point is
that the method of aggression determines the method of resistance: the veil's significance
was not intrinsic but assigned, and the assignment could be reversed by either party.

This episode is the reason arguments about the veil so rarely stay about the veil. Once
unveiling became the colonizer's project, veiling became available as sovereignty — which is
a political fact with no theological content, and which cuts against both the claim that
modern veiling is simple piety and the claim that it is simple submission.

## 4. The Revival

From the 1970s the veil returned, and it returned without state compulsion in most places
where it returned most decisively. The Egyptian figures are the ones usually cited: veiling
was close to absent among Cairo's urban women in 1969, and by 2000 a large majority of
Cairene women wore some form of it.

The scholarship identifies several drivers, and no single one carries the case.

- **Movements rather than states.** In Egypt the spread ran through universities, grassroots
  networks, and Islamist organizations. Young, educated women adopted it as a deliberate
  choice, framing it as a way to reconcile education and public participation with religious
  commitment
- **A broader religious resurgence.** The revival aimed to restore Islam to prominence, and
  produced legal, cultural, and devotional changes far beyond dress
- **Political failure.** The collapse of secular Arab nationalist credibility after 1967
  removed the main competitor, and the Iranian revolution's success in 1979 supplied a
  demonstration effect
- **Economic and demographic pressure.** Delayed development, rising inequality, reduced
  social mobility, and a large educated youth cohort with frustrated expectations are the
  structural conditions economists have modeled as conducive to revival

The revival is the single hardest fact for the argument that veiling is imposed, and the
reason the anthropological literature of section 6 exists. It is also, as Lazreg notes, not
evidence of spontaneity by itself, since promotional infrastructure accompanied it.

## 5. Forced Veiling

The second half of the pattern begins with the Iranian revolution.

**Iran.** Iranian women marched in Tehran on International Women's Day in March 1979 against
compulsory veiling, in one of the first mass protests against the new order. Hijab was made
obligatory for all women from April 1983, and the Islamic Penal Code of July 12, 1983
provided up to 74 lashes for appearing in public without it. The obligation applies to
non-Muslims and foreign visitors as well.

Mahsa (Jina) Amini died in the custody of the morality police in September 2022, setting off
the nationwide Woman, Life, Freedom protests and a durable collapse in compliance across parts
of Iranian society. The state's legislative answer, the Law to Support the Family by
Promoting Chastity and Hijab, passed parliament and was finalized in 2024, adding fines,
surveillance provisions, and escalating penalties. Its enforcement has been stalled since the
Supreme National Security Council formally asked parliament to suspend it on Dec. 14, 2024;
President Masoud Pezeshkian, elected in July 2024 partly on social freedoms, has said his
administration cannot implement it. Authorities have meanwhile pursued enforcement by other
means, and women in Tehran, Shiraz, and Isfahan have reported text-message warnings generated
from surveillance footage, which digital rights researchers attribute to facial recognition
cross-referenced against ID and mobile databases. As of 2025 officials maintained the hijab
laws remained in force while the new law stayed unenforced.

**Afghanistan.** In May 2022 the Taliban's supreme leader, Hibatullah Akhundzada, decreed the
burqa the preferred form of covering and made male relatives responsible for women's
compliance. The Law on Propagation of Virtue and Prevention of Vice, published in 2024,
requires women to cover face and body in public, bans transparent, tight, or short clothing,
and directs that women conceal their voices. A sweeping enforcement operation ran in Kabul
from late December 2023 into January 2024, with checkpoints and detentions; women described
detention lasting days or weeks with physical abuse. UN bodies raised concerns again in 2024
and 2025, and reporting through early 2026 describes an intensified crackdown, including
women barred from hospitals without a burqa.

**Turkey, in the other direction.** Turkey lifted its long-standing ban on headscarves in
most state institutions on Sept. 30, 2013, as part of a package presented as
democratization, initially excluding the military and judiciary; those exclusions were
subsequently relaxed. The Turkish sequence is the clearest single illustration that the
politics are about state power over women's dress rather than about the garment, since the
same state has compelled both directions within living memory.

## 6. Bans in the West and in India

- **France, 2004.** Law 2004-228 of March 15, 2004, effective that September, barred
  conspicuous religious symbols in public primary and secondary schools, framed as an
  application of *laïcité*. It applies to large crosses and *kippot* as well as headscarves,
  and Sikh students were also affected, but the debate was overwhelmingly about Muslim girls
- **France, 2010.** A law of October 2010 prohibited concealing the face in public space,
  directed in practice at the *niqab* and *burqa*
- **Strasbourg, 2014.** In *S.A.S. v. France*, decided July 1, 2014, the Grand Chamber of the
  European Court of Human Rights upheld the face-covering ban. The Court accepted that the
  prohibition interfered with freedom of religion and private life, but held France entitled
  to impose it to protect the rights and freedoms of others — the "living together"
  rationale, which has drawn sustained academic criticism as an elastic ground
- **India, 2022.** Karnataka's government order of Feb. 5, 2022, barred the hijab in state
  educational institutions with prescribed dress codes. The Karnataka High Court upheld it on
  March 15, 2022, holding that wearing the hijab is not an essential religious practice and
  so attracts no constitutional protection. On Oct. 13, 2022, a two-judge bench of the
  Supreme Court split: Justice Hemant Gupta dismissed the appeals, Justice Sudhanshu Dhulia
  allowed them and would have set the High Court judgment aside. The matter went to the Chief
  Justice for assignment to a larger bench, and the High Court ruling stands in the interim

The doctrinal move in the Karnataka judgment is worth noting, because a secular court decided
a question of Islamic law — whether the practice is essential to the religion — in order to
decide a question of constitutional law. That is the same question Part Three examines, asked
by a body with no standing in the tradition and every power over its adherents.

## 7. What Survey Data Shows

The best comparative data remains the University of Michigan Middle Eastern Values Survey,
fielded 2011 to 2013 and published by the Pew Research Center in January 2014. Respondents in
seven countries — Tunisia, Egypt, Iraq, Lebanon, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey — chose
from a card showing six styles of women's head covering.

Two findings, and they pull in different directions. In every country surveyed, the most
chosen option was full hair and ear covering without face covering — the middle position
described in Part Three, not either extreme. And substantial minorities to near-majorities
said women should decide for themselves, highest in Tunisia at 56 percent, Turkey at 52
percent, and Lebanon at 49 percent.

The instrument drew methodological criticism at the time for asking about "appropriate"
attire for women in a format that presumed the question was a public one. That criticism is
itself a datum: whether a woman's clothing is a matter for collective judgment is the
question underneath the whole debate, and a survey cannot ask it neutrally.

# Part Six: Source Criticism — Genre Determines What a Citation Proves

The works cited across this document are not interchangeable authorities. A verse, a screened
hadith collection, a legal manual, a state fatwa, an ethnography, and a polemic support
different kinds of claim, and most of the bad argument in this field comes from citing one as
though it were another.

## 1. The Genres, and What Each Can Support

| Source type | Examples used here | What a citation to it establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quranic text | 24:30-31, 33:53, 33:59, 24:60 | The wording of the command and its stated purpose. Not the scope of application, which requires interpretation |
| Classical *tafsir* | Reports from Ibn Abbas and Ibn Mas'ud on *illa ma zahara minha* | That early authorities disagreed, and how. Not a resolution — the disagreement is the finding |
| Screened hadith collections | Bukhari, Sunan Abi Dawud on the compliance reports | That the report met the compiler's admission standard, and that the practice was understood as commanded in the community that transmitted it |
| *Fiqh* manuals and *usul* works | The *awrah* rulings of the four schools | What jurists held and on what reasoning. Not what ordinary Muslims wore |
| State fatwa bodies | Dar al-Ifta, Al-Azhar | The current institutional position of a national authority. Authoritative for policy, not decisive as scholarship |
| Advocacy research institutes | Yaqeen Institute papers | A carefully assembled statement of the mainstream case. It is advocacy, and says so |
| Academic history | Leila Ahmed, D.A. Spellberg, Kecia Ali | What can be shown about how meanings and rules changed over time. Not whether a ruling binds |
| Ethnography | El Guindi, Mahmood, Bucar, Bullock | What the practice means and does for the population studied. Generalizes only as far as the sample |
| Feminist critique and polemic | Mernissi, Lazreg, Amin | The shape of an argument and the evidence marshalled for it. Mernissi's textual reading of 33:53 and her historical reconstruction are separately weighted |
| Human rights reporting | HRW, Amnesty, UN bodies, Iran International | Documented state conduct and legal texts. Strong on what governments did; not a source on what Islam requires |
| Court judgments | *S.A.S. v. France*; Karnataka HC and Indian Supreme Court | What a legal system decided and on what reasoning. No standing whatever on the religious question, even where the reasoning turns on it |

## 2. What This Does to the Arguments

Applying the distinction produces a finding against each side.

**Against the traditionalist consensus argument.** The claim of unbroken agreement is a claim
about *fiqh* manuals, and *fiqh* manuals record what jurists held, not what was practiced.
Part Two's evidence that full veiling with seclusion was for most of Islamic history an urban
elite practice — because seclusion required wealth, and because veiling tracked urbanization
— comes from social history, and it does not contradict the juristic consensus so much as
show what the consensus governed. The rule was agreed; the practice was uneven. Any argument
that moves from "the schools agreed" to "Muslim women have always covered" has changed genre
without noticing.

**Against the historicist argument.** Establishing that veiling predates Islam, that its
symbolism was refashioned under colonialism, and that its modern revival has identifiable
political and economic drivers is history, and history of this kind cannot reach a normative
conclusion. Every one of those findings is compatible with the obligation being binding. The
genealogical move — this practice has a compromising history, therefore it does not bind —
is not valid, and it is made frequently.

**Against citing ethnography as a settlement.** Fieldwork showing that women experience
covering as identity, cosmopolitanism, or release from a beauty economy is strong evidence
about meaning and no evidence about obligation. Fieldwork showing that women experience it as
constraint has exactly the same limits. Both are routinely deployed as though they answered
the juristic question, which neither can.

**Against citing Mernissi undifferentiated.** *The Veil and the Male Elite* contains two
arguments of very different strength. Her reading of 33:53 as an etiquette rule for one
household, addressed to men, is a textual claim that survives scrutiny and is accepted well
beyond her own camp. Her reconstruction of an interrupted egalitarian program abandoned under
Umar's pressure is a historical claim that reviewers have faulted for a narrow source base
and sweeping unattributed assertions. Citing the book as a single authority conflates the two.

**The usable rule.** Match the claim to the genre. A verse supports a claim about what was
commanded. A hadith collection supports a claim about what was transmitted and received. A
*fiqh* manual supports a claim about what jurists held. An ethnography supports a claim about
what a practice means to a population. A human rights report supports a claim about what a
state did. None of them supports a claim about what God requires of a woman in 2026 without
an argument connecting it — and the argument, not the citation, is where the disagreement
lives.
