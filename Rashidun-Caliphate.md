> **About this document.** The thirty years between the death of the Prophet Muhammad and the
> founding of the Umayyad dynasty, covering the succession crisis at Saqifah, the compilation of
> the Quran into a single text, and the reigns of the four caliphs Sunni tradition calls
> *Rashidun*, the rightly guided. The material was moved here on Aug. 26, 2026, from
> [Life-of-Muhammad.md](Life-of-Muhammad.md), where it had run on past the end of the Prophet's
> life. The caliphate of Umar ibn al-Khattab is summarized here and treated in full in
> [Umar-ibn-al-Khattab.md](Umar-ibn-al-Khattab.md). Sources are cited inline. The succession is
> the point at which the Sunni and Shia accounts of early Islamic history diverge, and where the
> two traditions read the same events differently, that is stated.

## 1. The Gathering at Saqifah

The Prophet died on 8 June 632 CE without naming a successor in terms the community agreed on.
While his immediate family prepared the body for burial, the leaders of the Medinan **Ansar**
gathered at a roofed meeting place called **Saqifah Bani Sa'idah** to elect a leader from among
themselves. Their reasoning was practical: a state with no head, in a peninsula full of tribes
that had submitted to a man rather than to an institution, would not last the week.

**Abu Bakr**, **Umar**, and **Abu Ubaidah** heard of the meeting and went straight there.

The argument ran hot. One proposal was a shared leadership, one chief from the Ansar and one
from the Muhajirun. Umar objected that a state cannot run on two heads.

Abu Bakr took the middle ground with some skill. He praised the Ansar for what they had
sacrificed, then made a political argument rather than a religious one: the tribes of Arabia
would accept the leadership of the Quraysh and would not accept anyone else, and holding the
peninsula together mattered more than which city the caliph came from. He then declined the post
for himself and proposed either Umar or Abu Ubaidah.

Umar refused to be placed above Abu Bakr, said **"Hold out your hand, O Abu Bakr,"** and pledged
allegiance to him. The Ansar leaders followed, then the rest of those present. The next day the
general public gathered at the Prophet's Mosque and took the pledge, making Abu Bakr the first
**Khalifah**, successor or deputy of the Messenger of Allah.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 86, Hadith 21; The History of al-Tabari, Vol. 9.

## 2. What the Two Traditions Make of It

Saqifah is the origin point of the oldest division in Islam, and the accounts of it are not
neutral.

* **The Sunni reading** treats the meeting as a consultation that arrived at the right answer.
  Abu Bakr was the senior companion, the Prophet had appointed him to lead the prayers during his
  final illness, and the pledge was general.
* **The Twelver Shia reading** holds that the Prophet had already designated **Ali ibn Abi
  Talib** as his successor, at Ghadir Khumm on the return from the Farewell Pilgrimage, and that
  the Saqifah meeting took place while Ali and the Prophet's family were occupied with the
  burial. On this account the succession was settled and then set aside.

Both traditions cite early material. This document follows the mainstream Sunni chronology
because that is what the source conversation supplied, and readers should treat the framing as a
position rather than a neutral record.

## 1. Before There Was a Book

During the Prophet's lifetime the Quran did not exist as a single bound volume, a *mushaf*. It
came in installments over twenty-three years. Companions memorized it as it arrived, and
designated scribes wrote it down on whatever was available: parchment, the shoulder blades of
camels, palm leaves, flat stones.

Two consequences follow. The text was distributed across hundreds of memories and hundreds of
scattered writing surfaces, and no single object held all of it.

## 2. Abu Bakr's Compilation, 633 CE

Shortly after Abu Bakr became caliph, the **Battle of Yamama** was fought against a tribal
rebellion. Around seventy of the **huffaz**, the men who held the whole text in memory, were
killed in it.

**Umar ibn al-Khattab** worked out the arithmetic and took it to Abu Bakr: a few more battles
like that one and portions of the Quran would exist only in writing that nobody had collected.
Abu Bakr resisted at first, on the ground that the Prophet had not done this and he was
reluctant to originate it. Umar kept pressing, and Abu Bakr agreed.

The job went to **Zayd ibn Thabit**, the Prophet's chief scribe. Zayd set a three-part standard,
and applied it to every verse:

1. It had to match his own memorization.
2. It had to be cross-checked against the memorization of other companions.
3. It had to be supported by **two independent written witnesses**, proving it had been
   transcribed in the Prophet's presence.

The result was a complete text on loose sheets, the **suhuf**. Custody passed from Abu Bakr to
Umar, and after Umar's death to his daughter **Hafsah bint Umar**, one of the Prophet's widows.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 66, Hadith 5010.

## 3. Uthman's Standardization, c. 650 CE

Two decades later the empire reached into Persia, Syria, and North Africa, and non-Arab converts
were learning the Quran from whichever reciter reached their province first. Regional differences
in pronunciation and dialect hardened, and in some garrison cities they turned into open
argument about which recitation was correct.

**Uthman ibn Affan** treated this as a threat to the text itself. He borrowed the *suhuf* from
Hafsah and put **Zayd ibn Thabit** back in charge of a committee to produce standardized copies.
Where dialect variants had to be resolved, the committee gave priority to the **Quraysh dialect**
the Prophet had spoken.

Master copies went to the principal cities of the empire, among them Mecca, Medina, Damascus,
Kufa, and Basra. Uthman then ordered every other partial or unverified manuscript destroyed, so
that no competing text would remain in circulation. Every Quran in use today descends from that
codex.

The destruction order is the part modern readers tend to catch on. It secured a single text at
the cost of the evidence that would let anyone now check the committee's work independently.
Both halves of that trade-off are in the sources.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 66, Hadith 5011.

# Part Three: Umar ibn al-Khattab, 634-644 CE

Umar's decade is the one in which a regional Arabian state became a transcontinental empire and
acquired the institutions to run it.

| Front | Years | Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Levant and Jerusalem** | 636-637 CE | Khalid ibn al-Walid broke the Byzantine army at **Yarmouk**. Patriarch Sophronius refused to surrender Jerusalem to anyone but the caliph, so Umar traveled there himself, took the keys, and issued the guarantee of Christian life, property, and worship. |
| **Persia** | 637-642 CE | The Sasanians were beaten at **Qadisiyyah** and broken at **Nihawand**, bringing Persia under Islamic rule. |
| **Egypt** | 640-642 CE | Amr ibn al-Aas took Alexandria and founded a new capital at **Fustat**, now part of Cairo. |

His administrative work mattered as much as the conquests: the **Diwan**, a state register and
pension system; the **Hijri calendar**, dated from the Hijrah; and a judiciary of appointed
**qadis** independent of the provincial military governors.

In 644 CE, while leading the dawn prayer in Medina, he was stabbed by **Abu Lu'lu'a Firuz**, a
Persian captive with a grievance over the tax he was made to pay.

**Umar's reign, his reforms, his treatment of non-Muslim subjects, and his death are covered in
full in [Umar-ibn-al-Khattab.md](Umar-ibn-al-Khattab.md).**

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 62, Hadith 48; The History of al-Tabari, Vol. 14.

# Part Four: Uthman ibn Affan, 644-656 CE

A six-man council appointed by Umar on his deathbed chose **Uthman ibn Affan** as third caliph.
His twelve years divide cleanly: the first half brought some of the largest territorial gains in
Islamic history, the second half brought the unrest that killed him.

## 1. Expansion and the First Muslim Navy

* **The navy.** Byzantine raiding along the coasts could not be answered from land. Uthman
  authorized his governor in Syria, **Muawiyah ibn Abi Sufyan**, to build the first Islamic
  fleet. It won a major engagement known as the **Battle of the Masts** in 655 CE, taking control
  of the eastern Mediterranean.
* **The territorial peak.** Under Uthman the empire pushed deep into North Africa, took Cyprus,
  reached into southern Europe, and extended east to the borders of modern Pakistan and
  Afghanistan.

## 2. The Grievances

* **Appointments.** Running an empire this size needed governors Uthman could trust, and he
  appointed experienced men from his own clan, the **Banu Umayya**. Senior companions and the
  garrison troops in Egypt, Kufa, and Basra read it as nepotism and saw themselves being cut out.
* **Money.** The pace of conquest slowed, and with it the flow of spoils that the provincial
  garrisons had come to count on as income.

## 3. The Assassination, 656 CE

In 656 CE an armed coalition of rebels from Egypt and Iraq marched on Medina and surrounded
Uthman's house. He would not let his defenders fight, refusing to have Muslim blood spilled over
him in the Prophet's city. The rebels broke in and killed him. He was reading a copy of the
Quran he had spent his reign standardizing.

The killing left no accepted mechanism for punishing the killers, which is the problem his
successor inherited.

> **Reference:** Sahih al-Bukhari, Book 62, Hadith 45; Kitab al-Tabaqat al-Kabir by Ibn Sa'd.

## 1. Order First or Retribution First

After Uthman's murder the public and a body of senior companions pledged allegiance to **Ali ibn
Abi Talib**, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law. He took over a state that had just watched its
head of state be killed by its own soldiers without consequence.

Ali's judgment was that order had to come first: stabilize the provinces, take control of state
revenue, replace Uthman's contested governors, and prosecute the killers once the state could
actually do it. A powerful faction, led by **Muawiyah**, governor of Syria and Uthman's kinsman,
took the opposite view and refused to recognize Ali's caliphate until Uthman's killers were
executed.

Neither position is unreasonable, which is why the argument turned into a war rather than a
verdict. This was the **First Fitna**, the first Islamic civil war.

## 2. The Battle of the Camel, 656 CE

An army led by the companions **Talha** and **Zubayr** and by the Prophet's widow **Aisha** met
Ali's forces near Basra, demanding justice for Uthman. Ali won. He treated Aisha with marked
respect and had her escorted safely back to Medina.

The battle is the first in which companions of the Prophet fought each other in the field, and
the tradition has never been comfortable with it.

## 3. The Battle of Siffin and the Arbitration, 657 CE

Ali's army met Muawiyah's Syrian army at **Siffin**. When Muawiyah's men saw the battle going
against them, they raised pages of the Quran on their spear points and called for the dispute to
be settled by arbitration under divine law. Ali accepted, to stop the killing.

Accepting was militarily costly and politically fatal. It converted a battlefield advantage into
a negotiation between equals, and it split his own army.

## 4. The Kharijites

A faction inside Ali's ranks held that submitting a matter of divine law to human arbitrators was
itself disbelief, and that Ali had committed it. They seceded, taking the name **Kharijites**,
the seceders, and turned on Ali. Their campaign extended to killing civilians who would not
accept their judgment.

Ali defeated them at **Nahrawan** in 658 CE. Enough survived to keep the movement alive.

## 5. The End of the Rashidun Era, 661 CE

In 661 CE a Kharijite, **Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam**, struck Ali with a poisoned sword as he
entered the mosque for the dawn prayer in **Kufa**, which Ali had made his capital. He died two
days later.

With him the era of caliphs chosen by consultation ended. **Muawiyah** took the caliphate and
passed it to his son, establishing the hereditary **Umayyad** dynasty.

> **Reference:** Sahih Muslim, Book 44, Hadith 48; The History of al-Tabari, Vols. 16 and 17.
